# Press Release and Media Kit

You are producing the official-record materials for a client's announcement. A press release is a journalist's raw material — structured so a reporter can lift facts, quotes, and context in minutes. A media kit removes every remaining excuse not to cover it. Follow AP style and the inverted-pyramid structure; deviations read as amateur.

## When a press release is (and isn't) the right tool

Reserve formal releases for genuine news: funding, launches, major hires/partnerships, milestones, acquisitions, awards, research/data. For softer angles, a personalized pitch (see the pitching skill) beats a release. A release with no news is noise. Often you'll write both: the release is the record; the pitch is what actually gets it opened.

## Structure (inverted pyramid — most important first)

1. **Header:** "FOR IMMEDIATE RELEASE" (or embargo line: "EMBARGOED UNTIL [date, time, timezone]"). Contact name, email, phone.
2. **Headline:** clear, specific, active, the news in one line — not a clever pun that hides the story. A subhead can add the key detail.
3. **Dateline + lead paragraph:** CITY, State/Country — Date — the lead answers **who, what, when, where, why** in 1–2 sentences. A reporter who reads only this must have the whole story. Lead with the news and its significance, not company boilerplate.
4. **Second paragraph — significance:** why it matters, the scale/impact, the context or trend it fits. Include the strongest data point.
5. **Quotes:** 1–2 quotes that say something real — insight or vision, not "We're thrilled/excited." One from a client exec, ideally one from a customer/partner/third party for credibility. Quotes must sound like a human said them and be pre-approved.
6. **Body:** supporting details, data, background, how it works — descending importance. A journalist should be able to cut from the bottom without losing essential facts.
7. **Boilerplate:** standard "About [Company]" paragraph — consistent across all releases.
8. **End mark:** "###" or "-30-" centered.

## Style rules (AP)

Third person, past/present tense, factual and neutral tone — no hype adjectives ("revolutionary," "game-changing," "world-class," "best-in-class"). Numbers: spell out one–nine, numerals for 10+. Dates, titles, and abbreviations per AP. Spell out acronyms on first use. One page ideal, two absolute max. Every claim must be true and verifiable — a false claim in a release is a legal and trust catastrophe. Superlatives ("first," "only," "largest") require proof.

## The media kit / press kit

Assemble a linkable kit (hosted page or shared folder) so reporters self-serve:
- The release (and any prior relevant releases).
- **Company fact sheet:** founding, leadership, size, key stats, one-line and one-paragraph descriptions.
- **High-resolution assets:** logo (multiple formats/backgrounds), product shots, executive headshots, screenshots, b-roll/video — all cleared for editorial use with usage rights stated.
- **Executive bios** for quotable spokespeople.
- **Boilerplate** copy-pasteable.
- **Data/research** referenced, with methodology if it's a data story.
- **Contact** for the PR lead, with real availability.
Make everything downloadable and correctly captioned/credited. A reporter on deadline who can't find a usable logo may just drop the story.

## Distribution reality

A newswire (PR Newswire, Business Wire, EIN) gives official distribution and SEO/coverage-of-record but rarely earns real journalist attention on its own — the personalized pitch does that. Use the wire for the record and reach; use targeted pitches for actual coverage. Time distribution with the pitch and any embargo.

## Quality bar

- Real news; inverted pyramid; the lead alone tells the story.
- AP style, neutral tone, zero hype adjectives, one page.
- Quotes say something; third-party quote for credibility; all approved.
- Every claim verifiable; superlatives proven.
- Media kit is complete, hosted, downloadable, rights-cleared, correctly captioned.

## Failure modes

- Boilerplate/hype in the lead instead of the news.
- Empty "thrilled to announce" quotes.
- Unverifiable superlatives ("world's first") → credibility and legal risk.
- Two-plus pages of marketing copy.
- Media kit with low-res or missing assets, or unclear usage rights.
- Relying on a wire blast for coverage without any real pitching.

**Great** looks like: a one-page, AP-clean release whose lead paragraph is the whole story, backed by substantive quotes and verifiable data, paired with a complete self-serve media kit — so a reporter can publish accurately in fifteen minutes.
