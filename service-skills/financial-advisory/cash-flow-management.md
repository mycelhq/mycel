# Cash-Flow Management

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. Most SMBs that fail are profitable on paper — they run out of cash. Your job is to give the owner a forward view of their bank balance and a set of levers to keep it positive. The core tool is the **13-week rolling cash-flow forecast**, the same instrument used in corporate turnarounds because it's short enough to be accurate and long enough to see trouble coming.

## Build the 13-week forecast

1. **Start from the actual bank balance today** (reconciled, not book), across all operating accounts.
2. **Lay out weekly columns for 13 weeks.** Weekly, not monthly — a business can be cash-positive for the month and overdrawn on the 12th when payroll hits before receivables land.
3. **Cash IN by week:** expected customer collections. Do not use invoice dates — use *expected payment dates* based on the A/R aging and each customer's actual payment behavior (a "Net 30" customer who always pays in 52 days gets 52). Add other inflows: loan draws, owner injections, tax refunds.
4. **Cash OUT by week:** payroll and payroll taxes (by actual pay dates), rent, loan payments, A/P by due date, recurring subscriptions, estimated taxes (the April/June/Sept/Jan dates), sales-tax remittance, and known one-offs.
5. **Compute the running balance.** Flag any week the balance dips below the owner's minimum operating cushion (define one — often one payroll cycle of cash).
6. **Roll it weekly:** each week drop the oldest column, add a new week 13, and — critically — compare last week's forecast to actuals to calibrate. Forecast accuracy improves only if you close the loop.

## Diagnose the cash conversion cycle (CCC)

CCC = Days Sales Outstanding (DSO) + Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO) − Days Payable Outstanding (DPO). It's how many days cash is tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Lowering it frees cash without borrowing.
- **DSO too high?** Slow collections. Levers: invoice immediately (not month-end), shorten terms, deposits/milestone billing, automated payment reminders at day 1/15/30, card/ACH on file, early-pay discounts (2/10 net 30), late fees, and stop-work triggers for chronic non-payers.
- **DIO too high?** Cash frozen in stock. Levers: reduce slow movers, just-in-time ordering, renegotiate minimums.
- **DPO too low?** Paying suppliers faster than necessary. Lever: negotiate longer terms, pay on the due date (not early) unless capturing a discount that beats the cost of capital.

## Managing a cash crunch (triage order)

When a week goes red: (1) accelerate collections — call the biggest overdue receivables personally; (2) stretch payables within terms and negotiate with key vendors; (3) delay discretionary spend; (4) time owner draws; (5) tap a line of credit *before* it's an emergency (banks lend to the solvent, not the desperate); (6) only then consider factoring/MCA — and warn the client that merchant cash advances are ruinously expensive (triple-digit effective APR) and usually a trap.

## Reserves and structure

Target an operating cash reserve of a defined number of months of fixed costs (commonly 3–6 for an SMB). Separate accounts for **taxes** (park sales tax and income-tax estimates the moment they're collected/earned — this money is not the client's) and payroll. Sweep operating surplus to reserve deliberately.

## Quality bar

**Great:** a live 13-week forecast calibrated weekly against actuals, expected-collection dates grounded in real customer behavior, a CCC diagnosis with quantified levers ("cutting DSO from 47 to 35 frees ~$18k"), separate tax/reserve accounts, and early-warning flags that give the owner weeks of lead time. **Acceptable:** a monthly cash forecast and a collections push. **Failing:** forecasting collections off invoice dates (fantasy inflows), ignoring the payroll/tax timing spikes, or discovering a shortfall the week it happens. The whole point is lead time — a forecast that only confirms today's overdraft has failed.
