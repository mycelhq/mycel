# Financial Forecasting & Budgeting

A budget is a plan expressed in dollars; a forecast is your best current estimate of where the numbers actually land. The client needs both: a budget to hold the year against, and a forecast that updates as reality diverges. Build them **driver-based**, not by nudging last year's lines up 5% — the value is in the assumptions being explicit and defensible.

## Build a driver-based model

Do not forecast revenue as a single growing line. Decompose it into the operational drivers the owner can actually influence:
- **Volume × price.** Units sold, customers, billable hours, subscriptions × average price/rate. For recurring-revenue businesses: starting base × (1 − churn) + new adds, tracked as MRR/ARR.
- **Pipeline-driven** for project businesses: leads × close rate × average deal size × timing.
Then the owner can ask "what if we raise price 8%?" or "what if churn drops to 3%?" and the model answers.

**Costs:** split fixed (rent, salaries, software, insurance) from variable (COGS, commissions, payment fees, hourly labor) that scale with revenue. Model variable costs as a % of revenue or per-unit so they flex automatically. Step costs (a new hire needed above X volume, a bigger space) should be explicit thresholds, not smooth lines.

## Three-statement integration

A credible forecast projects all three statements, linked:
1. **P&L** from the drivers above → net income.
2. **Cash flow** — because net income ≠ cash. Layer in A/R and A/P timing, inventory builds, capex, loan principal, taxes, and owner draws. This is where a "great year" can still mean a cash hole (growth consumes working capital).
3. **Balance sheet** rolls forward from the other two and must balance — it's your integrity check. If it doesn't balance, an assumption is inconsistent.

## Scenarios (never a single line)

Produce at least three: **base** (most likely), **downside** (revenue 20–30% light, key customer lost, sales cycle slips), and **upside**. For the downside, answer the survival questions: When does cash go negative? What's the minimum revenue to cover fixed costs (**breakeven** = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin)? What's the cash runway? The downside case is the most valuable one — it defines the covenants of the business.

## Sensitivity and unit economics

Identify the 2–3 assumptions the outcome is most sensitive to (usually price, volume, churn, or a major cost) and show how the answer moves as each flexes ±10–20%. Ground the model in unit economics: contribution margin per unit/customer, CAC, LTV, LTV:CAC ratio, payback period. If unit economics are broken, no volume forecast fixes it — flag it.

## Budget vs. actual (BvA) — the discipline that makes it real

A budget filed and forgotten is worthless. Each month after close, produce a BvA report: budget, actual, variance ($ and %), for every material line, with written explanations for meaningful variances. Then **re-forecast** the rest of the year from actuals (a rolling forecast) so the outlook stays honest. Distinguish timing variances (will reverse) from permanent ones (change the trajectory).

## Assumptions register

Every number in the model traces to a stated assumption: growth rate and why, price changes, hiring plan, collection days, tax rate, capex plan. When you present, lead with the assumptions — that's what the owner should challenge. A model whose assumptions are hidden inside formulas can't be steered or trusted.

## Quality bar

**Great:** driver-based, three-statement-integrated, three scenarios with breakeven and runway, sensitivity on the key drivers, an explicit assumptions register, and a monthly BvA + rolling re-forecast loop. Owner can run their own what-ifs. **Acceptable:** a driver-based P&L with a base/downside case and monthly variance tracking. **Failing:** last-year-plus-a-percent with no drivers, a P&L-only forecast that ignores working-capital cash needs, a single point estimate with no downside, or a budget never compared to actuals. The forecast's job is to be steered by — build it so decisions can be simulated before they're made.
