# Accountability and Progress

Insight in the session is worthless if nothing changes between sessions. The real work of coaching happens in the six or thirteen days between meetings, and it only happens if there's an accountability structure that makes action likely and follow-through visible. This is the machinery that separates coaching that changes lives from coaching that produces nice conversations.

## Design commitments that actually get done

At the close of every session, convert insight into a commitment the client is likely to keep:
- **Client-authored.** They propose the action in their words. A commitment you assign is one they can disown; one they chose, they own.
- **Specific and small enough to succeed.** "Have the hard conversation with Sam by Thursday" beats "communicate better." Prefer the smallest meaningful step over an ambitious one that stalls. Momentum from small wins compounds; a too-big commitment produces guilt and avoidance.
- **Time-bound with a first step.** When, and what's the very first move. Ask them to name it concretely.
- **Pre-mortem the obstacle.** Before they leave, ask "what might get in the way, and what will you do when it does?" Anticipated obstacles get handled; unanticipated ones become excuses. This single question dramatically raises follow-through.
- **Anchored to existing habits.** Tie the new behavior to an existing routine ("after your Monday standup, you'll…") — implementation intentions stick far better than free-floating intentions.

## Make progress visible

The client should be able to see they're moving. Build a lightweight tracking system:
- **A running commitments log** — what they committed to each session and whether it happened. Review it openly. Patterns emerge fast: what they consistently do vs. consistently avoid tells you where the real block is.
- **Re-measure against the baseline** at the mid-point and end (self-ratings, and stakeholder pulse for executive engagements). Progress the client can point to sustains motivation and justifies the engagement's value.
- **Capture wins explicitly.** Clients discount their own progress; part of your job is to name and bank the wins so they feel the momentum. "Three months ago you couldn't do X — look at now."

## Run the accountability conversation

Open every session by following up on the last commitment — never skip this. How you handle the two cases matters:
- **When they did it:** don't just move on. Explore what made it work, how it felt, what they learned. This reinforces the behavior and extracts the transferable lesson.
- **When they didn't:** this is coaching gold, not a failure to gloss over. Get curious, not disappointed. "What got in the way?" Usually it reveals the real block — the commitment was too big, the motivation was borrowed, or there's an underlying fear. Coach *that*. Never shame; shame produces hiding, and a client who hides is a client you can't help. But also don't collude — if a pattern of non-completion emerges, name it directly and make it the topic.

## Sustain change beyond the session

Behavior change fails when it depends on you. Build toward independence:
- **Between-session structure** where contracted: a brief weekly written reflection, a check-in message at a pre-agreed trigger point, or a simple habit tracker. Keep it light — heavy homework gets abandoned.
- **Build the client's own accountability muscle.** Over the engagement, shift ownership: have them set their own commitments, track themselves, and eventually run their own review. The goal is a client who no longer needs you.
- **Environmental design.** Coach them to change their environment, not just their willpower — calendar blocks, removing friction, enlisting a colleague, public commitments. Sustainable change is structural, not heroic.

## Handle stalls and plateaus

- If a client repeatedly doesn't follow through, stop pushing harder on the action and go up a level: is the goal still theirs? Is there a hidden competing commitment (e.g. "I say I want to delegate, but being needed is my identity")? Surface the competing commitment — that's usually the real work.
- Recalibrate cadence or commitment size if the current design isn't producing movement.

## Quality bar

- **Great:** every session ends in a client-owned, specific, obstacle-anticipated commitment; every session opens by reviewing the last one honestly; progress is tracked against a baseline and visibly building; wins are named; and over time the client is running their own accountability. There is documented behavior change, not just insight.
- **Failure:** vague or coach-assigned commitments, no follow-up on prior actions, no tracking, shaming missed commitments (or ignoring a pattern of them), and an engagement that produced good conversations but no observable change. If nothing is different in the client's actual life, the coaching didn't work — the accountability system is how you prevent that.
