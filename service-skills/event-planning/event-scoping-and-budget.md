# Event Scoping and Budget

An event fails at the scoping stage, not the day-of. Overpromise against the budget, misjudge the guest count, or start the timeline too late, and no amount of day-of heroics recovers it. Your job here is to make the money and the calendar honest before a single deposit is paid.

## Step 1: Define the event in one paragraph

Force clarity before creativity:
- **Objective and success metric.** A wedding's metric is the couple's felt experience; a product launch's is press/leads/attendance; a gala's is dollars raised. Name it. Every later trade-off is decided against this.
- **Guest count (the master variable).** Almost every cost scales with headcount — catering, rentals, venue capacity, staff ratios, favors. Get a realistic number with a confirmed vs. optimistic range. Never plan to the optimistic number.
- **Date + flexibility.** A fixed date removes negotiating leverage on venues; a flexible date (off-season, weekday, off-peak) can cut venue and vendor cost 20-40%.
- **Vibe and non-negotiables.** Three adjectives, plus the 2-3 things that must be true and the hard no-gos.

## Step 2: Build the budget from headcount, allocate by priority

Start from total budget and guest count → per-head figure. Then allocate. A typical full-service event distributes roughly:
- Venue + catering/beverage: 45-55% (the biggest lever by far)
- Rentals, furniture, AV/production: 10-20%
- Décor/florals: 10-15%
- Photography/video: 8-12%
- Entertainment/talent: 5-10%
- Staffing, coordination, planning fee: 10-15%
- **Contingency: 10-15%, always, untouched until day-of.** An event with no contingency is a crisis waiting for a rain delay or a no-show vendor.

Percentages flex by event type — adjust, but make every dollar a deliberate choice tied to the priority ranking. Have the client rank what matters (e.g. food over flowers), and fund the ranking. Show them the trade-offs explicitly: "To get the band you want, we drop the plated dinner to stations."

## Step 3: Pressure-test hidden costs

The line items that blow budgets because amateurs forget them:
- Service charge + gratuity on catering (often 18-25% on top of the food/bev subtotal — a huge number).
- Tax on everything.
- Rentals nobody pictures: linens, glassware, restrooms for outdoor sites, generators, heaters/fans, tenting, dance floor, staging.
- Insurance / liability coverage and any required permits (alcohol, amplified sound, road/park use, fire marshal for capacity).
- Overtime rates if the event runs long — know the cutoff and the hourly.
- Vendor meals, parking, load-in/out fees, and delivery/pickup charges.
- Setup/strike labor and the time window the venue actually allows.

Walk the client through these before they fall in love with a plan they can't afford.

## Step 4: Build the critical-path timeline (backwards)

Work back from the event date and identify long-lead items — the things that gate everything else:
- **12+ months (large events):** lock venue and date. Everything depends on the venue.
- **9-12 months:** book the vendors that sell out first — top caterers, photographers, bands, and popular venues. Send save-the-dates for weddings.
- **6-9 months:** design/décor direction, secondary vendors, room blocks, invitations.
- **3-6 months:** menu tasting, rentals, timeline draft, RSVP tracking begins.
- **1-2 months:** finalize headcount, seating, run-of-show, confirm every vendor, final payments scheduled.
- **Final 2 weeks:** confirmations, contingency plans, day-of packet.

The rule: identify what has the longest lead and least availability, and book it first. A venue booked late collapses the whole timeline.

## Step 5: Lock scope in writing

Produce a scope document: event definition, guest range, itemized budget with contingency, priority ranking, timeline with milestones, and an explicit list of what's included vs. excluded and who owns what (you vs. client vs. vendors). Get sign-off. This is your contract against "can we just add…" — every addition trades against the budget or the contingency, and you say so out loud.

## Quality bar

- **Great:** the budget is built from a realistic headcount, every hidden cost is surfaced early, 10-15% contingency is protected, long-lead vendors are booked first, and the client understood every trade-off before committing. Nothing is a surprise later.
- **Acceptable:** minor reallocations mid-planning, absorbed by contingency.
- **Failure:** planning to the optimistic guest count, forgetting service charge/tax/rentals, no contingency, or starting the timeline so late the good vendors are gone. These are scoping failures that no day-of coordination can fix.
