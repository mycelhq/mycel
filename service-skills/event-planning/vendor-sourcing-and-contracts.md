# Vendor Sourcing and Contracts

Vendors are the event. A great plan executed by a no-show caterer or an uninsured venue is a disaster. Your job is to assemble a reliable roster, lock terms that protect the client, and leave zero ambiguity about who delivers what, when.

## Sourcing: build a shortlist that fits

For each category, source 2-3 real candidates — never single-source a critical vendor.
- Match to the event's style, budget tier, and guest count. A caterer great at 40-person plated dinners may fail at 300 stations.
- Prioritize by lead time and scarcity: book the scarce ones (top caterers, photographers, bands, venues) first, before they sell the date.
- Prefer vendors with a track record at your specific venue or event type — they know the load-in, the power, the quirks.

## Vetting: separate reliable from risky

Before you shortlist, verify:
- **Reviews and references** — recent, and ideally for events of your size. Ask references specifically: did they show up on time, did anything go wrong, how did they handle it?
- **Insurance and licensing** — liability insurance is mandatory; the venue will likely require a COI naming them as additional insured. Alcohol service needs the proper license. No insurance, no booking.
- **Capacity and backup** — what happens if the lead photographer is sick, the truck breaks down, a key staffer quits? A professional has a documented backup plan. Ask.
- **Portfolio depth** — one great highlight reel hides a thin body of work. Look for consistency across full galleries, not just the best shots.
- **Responsiveness** — a vendor slow to reply during sales will be slower during a crisis. Treat response time as a data point.

## Getting comparable quotes

- Send every candidate the same detailed brief: date, headcount, timeline, venue, exact scope, and any constraints. Vague briefs get vague quotes you can't compare.
- Demand itemized quotes, not a lump sum. You cannot negotiate or spot gaps in a single number.
- Normalize before comparing: one caterer's price may exclude service charge, gratuity, rentals, or staff that another includes. Build an apples-to-apples comparison so the "cheap" quote isn't actually the expensive one.

## Negotiation levers

- **Off-peak:** weekday, off-season, or daytime dates unlock real discounts. Flexibility is your best lever.
- **Bundling:** a venue that provides catering/AV in-house, or a vendor who does both ceremony and reception, often discounts the package.
- **Value over price:** negotiate added value (extra hour, upgraded linens, second shooter) when they won't cut the number — often easier for the vendor to grant and worth more to the client.
- **Payment terms:** stagger deposits and final payments to protect the client's cash and create leverage — never pay 100% upfront.
- Be firm but fair. You'll rebook these vendors; a scorched relationship costs future events.

## Contracts: the details that prevent disasters

Never operate on a verbal agreement or an emailed quote. A real contract specifies:
- **Exact scope and deliverables** — hours of coverage, headcount, menu, number of staff, specific items. Ambiguity is billed against the client later.
- **Date, times, load-in/out windows, and location.**
- **Total price with every fee itemized** — service charge, gratuity, tax, overtime rate, travel, delivery. No "TBD."
- **Payment schedule** and what triggers each payment.
- **Cancellation and refund policy** — both sides. What if the client cancels? What if the vendor does?
- **Force majeure / postponement** — critical post-2020; know what happens if the event can't proceed (weather, illness, venue closure) and whether deposits transfer.
- **Insurance/COI requirements** and liability allocation.
- **Substitution clause** — can they swap your named lead photographer for someone else? Get the right to approve.
- Read every contract line by line before the client signs. Flag anything one-sided.

## Coordination and accountability

- Maintain a master vendor list: company, contact name, cell phone, arrival time, load-in window, scope, balance due, and COI status.
- Confirm every vendor in the final 1-2 weeks: date, time, address, scope, and point of contact. Re-confirm arrival times the day before.
- Make yourself (or the day-of coordinator) the single point of contact so vendors aren't calling a stressed client.

## Quality bar

- **Great:** 2-3 vetted, insured candidates per category compared on itemized apples-to-apples quotes; contracts specify scope/fees/cancellation/force majeure/substitution; every vendor confirmed twice with a backup plan on file; the client paid a fair price with no surprise fees.
- **Failure:** single-sourcing, booking on a verbal quote, missing COIs, discovering service charge/overtime/rentals weren't included, or a vendor no-show with no backup. These are sourcing and contracting failures, and they surface on the worst possible day.
