# Day-of Coordination

Day-of is where months of planning meet reality. Your single mandate: the client and their guests experience the event; you absorb every problem before it reaches them. A great coordinator is invisible to guests and indispensable to everyone working the event. Nothing below is improvised — it's the execution of the run-of-show under live conditions.

## Before doors: own the setup

- Arrive first, at load-in start. Walk the space against the floor plan and ROS.
- Check in every vendor as they arrive against the master list: right vendor, right time, right scope. Direct them to their zone and confirm their cues.
- Verify the critical path is on schedule: staging/power → AV → sound check → tables → linens → florals/finishing. If something's behind, reshuffle now while there's slack, not at doors.
- Run the **full sound/AV check** with real mics, slides, and playlists before a single guest is in the room. Test every wireless mic (and its batteries), every input, the presentation clicker, the music handoff.
- Walk the guest journey yourself: parking → entrance → coat/check-in → first space. Fix anything a guest would trip on — signage, a dark corner, a blocked path, missing menus.
- Confirm the timeline with the key players (MC, band/DJ, caterer captain, photographer) — same first-dance time, same dinner call, same toast order.

## During: run the show, don't watch it

- **Work the ROS as a live conductor.** Track against the clock. Cue each transition on its trigger — the toasts, the dinner service, the headliner, the send-off. You initiate; you don't wait and hope.
- Communicate on radio/text with vendor leads. Give the "5-minute warning" before every major transition so the band, catering, and photographer are ready simultaneously — the classic failure is one vendor not knowing the moment arrived.
- **Absorb problems silently.** The cake is late, a vendor is lost, a mic died, a guest is upset — you solve it in the background. The client should never see you stressed or handling a crisis; the moment you pull them in, they stop experiencing their event.
- Keep the energy managed: watch for dead air and cue music or the host to fill it; read the room and flex flexible segments if things run long or short (per the ROS's pre-decided flex points).
- Manage the client and VIPs gently: keep them on their marks for key moments (photos, entrance, toast) without making them feel handled.

## The emergency kit and the fixer mindset

Carry a kit — it prevents small problems from becoming visible ones: safety pins, sewing kit, stain remover, scissors, tape, extra batteries, phone chargers/cables, gaffer tape, zip ties, pain relievers, band-aids, breath mints, a printed contact sheet and ROS, cash for tips/emergencies, backup copies of the timeline and floor plan, a lint roller, and a multi-tool.

The fixer mindset: for any problem, decide fast — can I fix it invisibly, do I trigger a pre-planned contingency, or does it genuinely require the client's decision (rare)? Default to the first. Most "disasters" are 90-second fixes if you stay calm and have the kit.

## Decisions and the client firewall

- You are the single point of contact. Vendors, venue staff, and guests route questions to you, not the client. This firewall is the whole value of day-of coordination.
- Pre-agree which decisions are yours (almost all) and the tiny set that need the client (major unbudgeted spend, safety calls). Everything else, you own.
- Make the weather/contingency call at the pre-set threshold and time — decisively.

## Strike and closeout

- Manage teardown against the venue's strike deadline to avoid overtime charges. Sequence vendor pickups so the dock isn't gridlocked.
- Collect the client's valuables, gifts, and personal items — a common thing to lose in the rush.
- Do a final sweep of the space for left-behind property and to confirm the venue is returned as required (damage deposit protection).
- Confirm final vendor balances/tips are handled per plan.
- Close the loop with vendors — thank the good ones; note issues for future sourcing.

## Quality bar

- **Great:** setup finished ahead of doors with a real sound check, every vendor checked in and cued, transitions hit on time via 5-minute warnings, problems solved invisibly, the client never touched a logistics decision, energy stayed up with no dead air, and strike beat the deadline. Guests saw a seamless event; the client attended their own party.
- **Failure:** a rushed or skipped sound check, a vendor missing their cue, dead air, the client dragged into fixing problems, a scramble with no emergency kit, or overtime charges from a chaotic strike. Day-of is pure execution — the plan is only as good as the person running the clock.
