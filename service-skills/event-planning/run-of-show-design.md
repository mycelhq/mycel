# Run-of-Show Design

The run-of-show (ROS) is the master timeline that turns a plan into a lived experience. It answers, for every minute: what is happening, who makes it happen, and what has to be ready. A great event feels effortless to guests precisely because the ROS eliminated every gap and collision behind the scenes.

## Two documents, not one

- **Production schedule (crew-facing):** load-in through strike, including vendor arrivals, setup, sound checks, and teardown. Hour-by-hour, sometimes minute-by-minute at peaks.
- **Run-of-show (event-facing):** the guest experience from doors-open to send-off, with cues, transitions, and the person responsible for each.

Both live in one master document, cross-referenced, distributed to every vendor and staff lead.

## Build the guest experience first, backward from anchors

Identify the fixed anchor moments (ceremony time, keynote, dinner service, headliner set, cake, toasts, send-off) and build around them. Then sequence to control energy:
- **Arrival buffer.** Guests never arrive on time; build a welcome/cocktail window so late arrivals don't derail the first anchor. Never schedule the most important moment at the literal start.
- **Energy arc.** Sequence to build: welcome → warm-up → peak (the headliner, the reveal, the first dance) → wind-down/send-off. Don't peak too early or you spend the rest of the night declining.
- **Feed people before you ask anything of them.** Hungry guests are restless; program speeches and asks after food hits, not before.
- **Kill dead air.** Every transition is a risk — the gap between dinner and dancing, the reset between sessions. Fill it with music, a host cue, a B-roll reel, a bar. Dead air is where energy dies and guests check phones or leave.
- **Respect attention limits.** No speech block over ~15-20 minutes; no session without a break. Cap the toasts and hold speakers to time.

## Write the ROS at the right resolution

Each row: **time | duration | what happens | who owns it | cue/trigger | notes/dependencies.**
- Time the whole thing realistically, then add buffer. Transitions, group photos, and speeches always run long. A schedule with no slack is a schedule that collapses at the first delay.
- Name a specific owner for every line — "DJ announces first dance," "banquet captain clears entrées," "coordinator cues photographer." A line nobody owns doesn't happen.
- Specify **cues and triggers**: what signals the next thing to start. Music change, MC line, lighting shift, the coordinator's radio call. The transitions are where events break — over-specify them.

## Production schedule: the invisible half

- Sequence load-in by dependency: staging and power before AV, AV before sound check, tables before linens before place settings, florals near the end so they're fresh.
- Respect the venue's access window — many charge overtime or lock out early. Confirm the real load-in start and the hard strike deadline.
- Schedule a **full sound/AV check well before doors** with the actual mics, slides, and playlists — not a rushed test as guests arrive. Test every wireless mic and every input.
- Build vendor arrival times with staggering so the loading dock isn't gridlocked and each vendor has what they depend on ready.

## Contingency baked in

For each risk, pre-decide the response in the ROS itself:
- **Weather** (outdoor): the call time and threshold for the rain plan, and who makes the call.
- **Runs long/short:** which segments can flex or be cut, and who decides live.
- **No-show/late vendor or speaker:** the backup and the reshuffle.
- **Medical/emergency:** location of first aid, exits, and the point person.
Contingencies decided in advance are a calm adjustment; decided live, they're a crisis.

## Distribution and alignment

- Every vendor gets the ROS with their parts highlighted and confirms their arrival time and cues.
- Hold a final walkthrough or call so all leads share the same timeline. The band, the caterer, and the photographer must agree on when the first dance is — misalignment here is a classic disaster.
- Give the MC/host an annotated script version so their announcements trigger the right cues.

## Quality bar

- **Great:** a minute-by-minute ROS with an owner and cue for every moment, realistic timing plus buffer, energy that builds and never dies in dead air, a production schedule sequenced by dependency with a real sound check, and pre-decided contingencies — so the day feels seamless to guests and calm to crew.
- **Failure:** a vague timeline with no owners or cues, no buffer so one delay cascades, dead air between segments, a rushed sound check, vendors working from different versions, or a contingency invented in a panic mid-event. The ROS is the single most leveraged document you produce — treat it as the deliverable it is.
