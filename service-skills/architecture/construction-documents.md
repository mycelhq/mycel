# Construction Documents

Construction Documents (CDs) are the contractual instructions that get the building priced, permitted, and built. This phase converts the approved design into precise, coordinated drawings and specifications. The measure of a good CD set is downstream and unforgiving: few RFIs, few change orders, no "it can't be built that way" in the field, and a bid that matches the budget. Ambiguity in CDs becomes the contractor's contingency (higher bid) or the owner's change order (higher cost).

## Coordinate across disciplines — the core discipline

CDs are multi-disciplinary: architectural, structural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, fire protection, civil, landscape. The single biggest source of field problems is **inter-discipline conflict** — a duct running through a beam, a light fixture where a sprinkler goes, a plumbing stack with no chase, a structural column in a doorway.

- Run **clash detection** (BIM/Revit coordination or careful overlay) and resolve conflicts on paper, where they're cheap. A clash caught in coordination costs an hour; the same clash caught in the field costs a change order and a delay.
- Hold coordination reviews with the consultants and reconcile every overlap: structural depths vs ceiling heights, MEP routing vs structure, shaft and chase sizing, roof penetrations, and slab openings.
- Keep the model/drawings the single source of truth so a change in one discipline propagates rather than diverging.

## Draw for buildability and completeness

- **Dimensions and datums**: dimension everything a builder needs, from consistent datums, with no gaps and no contradictions. Every wall located, every opening sized and placed, floor-to-floor and finish elevations defined.
- **Details**: draw the conditions that matter — wall sections, envelope assemblies, waterproofing and flashing, roof edges, thresholds, connections, and any non-standard junction. The details are where water intrusion, thermal bridging, and constructability are won or lost. Show how the building keeps water out and heat in.
- **Schedules**: complete door, window, room-finish, and equipment schedules, cross-referenced to plans and details.
- **Assemblies and ratings**: call out fire-rated assemblies, acoustic assemblies, and their tested listings.
- **Reference discipline**: consistent sheet numbering, detail callouts that actually point to real details, and matching tags across sheets. A callout to a detail that doesn't exist is an instant RFI.

## Write clear specifications

Specifications define quality, materials, and workmanship that drawings can't. Use a recognized structure (CSI MasterFormat divisions) so contractors and estimators find things where they expect. For each material and system, specify product standards, performance requirements, tolerances, installation standards, and submittal/testing requirements. Decide and state the procurement basis — proprietary (named product), or performance/or-equal. Avoid the two classic spec failures: **conflicts between the specs and the drawings** (which control? state it), and **boilerplate for systems not in this project** (which confuses bidders and invites claims). Coordinate specs to drawings deliberately.

## Keep it constructible and on budget

- **Constructability**: design to available materials, standard sizes, and real construction sequences. Ask "who builds this, in what order, and can they reach it?" An elegant detail no trade can actually execute is a defect.
- **Cost control**: update the cost estimate at CD milestones (e.g. 50%, 90%, 100%). If the set is drifting over budget, value-engineer deliberately before bidding, not after bids come in high. Redesigning after over-budget bids wastes the whole set's momentum.
- **Code and permit alignment**: ensure the CDs carry through everything permitting required and match the approved design — the permit set and the construction set must not diverge.

## Prepare for bidding and construction admin

Issue a complete, coordinated set that a contractor can bid from without guessing. Anticipate the questions a bidder will ask and answer them in the documents. During bidding, handle addenda cleanly (all bidders get the same information). Set up for the construction phase: an RFI process, submittal review expectations, and a clear line on what constitutes a change. A low RFI/change-order count later is the truest grade of the set.

## Quality bar and failure modes

Great: fully coordinated across disciplines with clashes resolved on paper, complete and unambiguous drawings and specs, buildable details, aligned with permit and budget, and a set the contractor prices tightly and builds with few RFIs or change orders. Acceptable: complete, code-compliant, biddable set with minor gaps. Failing: uncoordinated disciplines producing field clashes, missing or contradictory dimensions, callouts to nonexistent details, specs that conflict with drawings or describe systems not in the project, un-constructible details, a set over budget discovered only at bid, or a construction set that diverges from the permitted design.
