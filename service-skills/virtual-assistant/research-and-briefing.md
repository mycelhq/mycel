# Research and Briefing

The client asked a question or is walking into something (a meeting, a purchase, a hire, a market). Your job is to compress the open web and any provided documents into a briefing that lets them decide or show up prepared — without reading everything you read.

## Scope before you search

Pin down the deliverable in one line before touching a search box: *What decision or moment does this feed, and what's the format and deadline?* A "quick look at competitors" for a sales call is different from a due-diligence memo. If the ask is vague, infer the most likely use and state your assumption at the top of the brief so the client can correct it.

Define success criteria: how many options, what dimensions to compare, what budget or constraints, what the client already knows (don't re-tell them the obvious).

## Search method

- **Triangulate.** Never rest a material claim on one source. For a fact that drives a decision (price, a person's role, a company's funding, a legal rule), get two independent confirmations. Prefer primary sources: the company's own site, filings, official docs, the person's LinkedIn/bio, the actual product page — over aggregators and listicles.
- **Date-check everything.** Note the publish date of each source. Flag anything older than ~18 months as potentially stale, especially prices, headcounts, and features. State "as of <date>" on time-sensitive facts.
- **Follow the money and the incentive.** A vendor's own comparison page is marketing; a review site may be affiliate-driven. Weight sources by their incentive to mislead.
- **Go deep enough, then stop.** Diminishing returns hit fast. Once your top two or three sources agree and you can answer the client's question, stop searching and start writing.

## For person/company prep (meetings)

Build a one-page dossier: who they are, current role and tenure, what their company does and its recent news (funding, launches, layoffs, leadership changes in the last ~6 months), mutual connections or shared background, likely priorities and pain points, and 2–3 smart questions the client could ask. Include one genuinely useful "you probably didn't know" fact. Never pad with generic bio filler.

## For options/purchase research

Build a comparison table: options as rows, the client's decision dimensions as columns (price, fit-for-purpose, key trade-offs, deal-breakers). End with a clear recommendation and a one-sentence why, plus the runner-up and when you'd pick it instead. The client should be able to overrule you, but they shouldn't have to do the synthesis themselves.

## The briefing format

Lead with the answer. Structure top-down:

1. **Bottom line** — the recommendation or the single most important finding, in 1–2 sentences.
2. **Key points** — 3–6 bullets, each a claim + the evidence, most decision-relevant first.
3. **Details/table** — the comparison or supporting facts, skimmable.
4. **Sources** — linked, with dates, so the client can verify or go deeper.
5. **Open questions / caveats** — what you couldn't confirm and what would change the answer.

Length matches stakes: a quick question gets 5 bullets; a real decision gets a page. Never bury the lede, never make the client read to the bottom to learn what you think.

## Quality bar and failure modes

Great: the client reads the first two lines, agrees or adjusts, and moves — and every claim is traceable to a dated source. Acceptable: accurate findings, clearly organized, sourced. Failing: a confident claim that's wrong or stale, a "recommendation" with no reasoning, a wall of text with no bottom line, or fabricated specifics.

Specific traps:
- **Hallucinated precision** — never invent a price, a date, a stat, or a quote. If you can't confirm it, say "couldn't confirm" rather than guessing. A plausible-looking wrong number is worse than an honest gap.
- **One-source syndrome** — a single blog post is a lead, not a finding.
- **Answering the literal question but missing the real one** — if they ask "how much is X" before a negotiation, also surface what X actually costs competitors and where the leverage is.
- **Stale-as-fresh** — presenting last year's pricing as current. Always timestamp.
