# Pre-Production Planning

Pre-production is where the video is actually won or lost. A shoot day costs the client thousands per hour in crew, talent, and location; your job is to arrive with zero open questions. Do not proceed to a shoot until every artifact below is locked and signed off.

## Step 1: Extract the real brief

Never accept "make us a promo video" at face value. Interrogate for:
- **One objective, measurable.** "Drive demo signups from the homepage," not "increase awareness." If the client can't name the single action a viewer should take, you don't have a brief yet.
- **Distribution first, creative second.** Where does this live — pre-roll YouTube ad, LinkedIn feed, trade-show loop, sales-email embed? Format follows placement: a 6-second bumper and a 3-minute case study are different products. Get exact aspect ratios (16:9, 9:16, 1:1) and max durations per placement.
- **Audience and the one belief to change.** Name the viewer and the before/after belief state.
- **Mandatories and no-gos.** Brand colors, logo lockups, legal disclaimers, competitor mentions, must-include exec/customer.

Write this as a one-page brief. Get explicit written approval before scripting. This document is your defense against scope creep.

## Step 2: Script and message architecture

- Open on tension or payoff in the first 3 seconds — social viewers decide to keep watching before any logo. Never open on a logo animation.
- Structure: hook → stakes → proof → resolution → single CTA. One CTA only.
- Write to duration. Budget ~150 spoken words per minute; a 60-second video is ~150 words max including breathing room. Read it aloud with a stopwatch.
- For talking-head/testimonial, write a **question guide**, not a script — real people can't deliver scripted lines. Prepare 8-12 questions engineered to produce the soundbites you need.

Deliver the script in a two-column format: VIDEO (what we see) | AUDIO (VO, dialogue, music, SFX). Lock the script with the client before building the shot list.

## Step 3: Shot list and storyboard

Convert every script line into concrete shots. Each shot row: shot number, scene, description, framing (WS/MS/CU/ECU), camera movement (static/pan/dolly/handheld), lens, location, talent, props, audio notes, estimated setup time.

- Storyboard anything with choreography, VFX, or a specific composition the client is imagining. For simple talking-head, frames are optional; for a branded ad, they are mandatory — they prevent the "that's not what I pictured" conversation in the edit.
- Always list **B-roll and cutaways** explicitly. Editors are starved for coverage; over-shoot inserts, hands, environment, product detail. Rule of thumb: 3x more B-roll than you think you need.
- Flag every shot that requires special gear (gimbal, drone, macro, slider) so it hits the equipment list.

## Step 4: Logistics that kill shoots if missed

- **Location:** scout in person or via photos/video at the actual shoot time-of-day. Check power outlets, ambient noise (HVAC, traffic, aircraft paths, nearby construction), natural light direction, ceiling height, and whether you need a permit or COI (certificate of insurance). Interiors near windows change dramatically hour to hour — note it.
- **Talent:** confirm availability with a buffer, send call times, wardrobe guidance (no tight patterns/stripes that moiré, no logo'd clothing unless intended), and signed **appearance/model releases** before rolling. No release, no usable footage.
- **Permissions:** location release, music license, and rights for any third-party assets. Never plan to "fix licensing later."
- **Schedule:** build a shoot schedule ordered by location and lighting, not script order. Group all shots at one setup together. Front-load must-have shots in case you lose time. Add 25% contingency to every time estimate — first-timers always underestimate setup/reset.

## Step 5: The production book

Assemble everything into one shareable document the whole crew reads: brief, script, shot list, storyboard, schedule with call times, location addresses + parking, contact sheet (every crew/talent phone number), equipment list, and a weather/backup plan for exteriors.

## Quality bar

- **Great:** on shoot day, the crew works from the book, every shot is pre-decided, the client has already approved the creative, and the only variable is performance. You finish with time to spare and extra coverage.
- **Acceptable:** minor on-the-fly adjustments but no reshoots needed.
- **Failure:** discovering on set that you're missing a release, the location is too loud, the script runs long, or the client "expected something different." Every one of these is a pre-production miss, not a bad-luck event.

Before declaring pre-production done, confirm: signed brief, locked script, complete shot list, confirmed talent with releases, secured location with permits/COI, licensed music, full schedule with call times, and a backup plan. If any is open, you are not ready.
