# The cold opener that gets a reply

The first message is not where you sell. It is where you earn the right to a second message. Its only job is to get a reply, and a reply comes from one feeling: "this was written to me, by a person, and it's easy to answer." Everything below serves that.

## The shape that works

One or two sentences. Under 300 characters — LinkedIn's own note cap sits near there, and anything longer reads as a pitch someone pasted. A specific observation or question, then nothing else. No ask for a call, no calendar link, no "I help X do Y," no pitch of the offer at all.

The strongest openers do exactly one of these:
- **Name something true about their world** that only someone paying attention would say. Their role plus a real tension of that role ("Running RevOps at a Series B usually means you're the person everyone blames for the number and nobody staffs — is that the shape of it there?").
- **Ask one genuine, answerable question** about how they do a specific thing, where you actually want the answer. Curiosity is disarming; a question that costs them 20 seconds to answer gets answered.
- **Give before taking** — a one-line observation, a relevant resource named (not linked), a genuine compliment about a specific thing they shipped (never generic flattery).

## What kills the reply

- **"I help [audience] achieve [outcome]."** This is the single most common opener and it is instantly recognized as a sales script. Never use it.
- **The calendar ask.** "Open to a quick call?" in message one converts near zero and marks you as a bot. The call is earned two or three exchanges in.
- **Flattery with no specifics** ("Love what you're doing!") — reads as mass-send, because it is.
- **Buzzwords, emojis, hashtags, links.** Each one lowers reply rate. Plain text from a real person outperforms every time.
- **Making them do work to understand it.** If they have to re-read it, they close it.

## Ground it in two things

A good opener is grounded in (1) what THIS founder actually sells and to whom, and (2) the recipient's actual world. You are not writing a generic template — you are writing the one line this founder would write to this kind of person if they had all day. Use the founder's real offer in plain words, and reference the recipient's role/industry/tension as a real person would, not as a merge field.

Use the literal token `{first_name}` once, where the name goes, and never invent a name.

## Great vs acceptable vs failing

- **Great:** a specific, human line that could only have been sent to this kind of person, that a busy stranger answers in one tap. It sounds like the founder, not like marketing.
- **Acceptable:** grounded and non-salesy, but slightly generic — true of many people in the ICP rather than pointed.
- **Failing:** any pitch, any call-to-action, any "I help," any flattery, anything over 300 characters, anything that needs a second read. If it feels like it was sent to a list, it was, and it will be ignored or reported.

## The test before you send

Read it as the recipient. Would a busy person who gets 30 of these a week reply to this one? If the honest answer is "they'd have to think about it," cut until the answer is yes. When in doubt, ask one real question and stop.
