# Brand Voice & Messaging Framework

You are building the verbal identity that every future piece of copy inherits. Without it, each page sounds like a different company and the brand blurs into the category. Messaging (what we say) and voice (how we say it) are separate deliverables — build both.

## Messaging: the strategic foundation

Before voice, lock the substance. Produce a **messaging framework** containing:

- **Positioning statement**: for [audience] who [need], [brand] is the [category] that [key differentiator], unlike [alternative]. This forces a real, defensible distinction — not "we're the best." If the differentiator would be equally true of a competitor, it's not one.
- **Value proposition**: the single most compelling reason to choose this brand, in plain language, outcome-first. One sentence a customer would actually say.
- **Message pillars** (3–4): the core themes every piece of communication reinforces (e.g. "radically simple," "built for teams," "your data stays yours"). Each pillar gets a proof point — the evidence that makes it credible. These are the reusable arguments all copy draws from.
- **Audience & their language**: who you're talking to, what they care about, and the actual vocabulary they use (from research — reviews, calls, community). Copy should speak their language, not the company's internal jargon.
- **Elevator pitches** at three lengths (one line / one paragraph / one minute) so anyone can represent the brand consistently.

## Voice: the consistent personality

Voice is constant; tone flexes by context. Define voice with **dimensions on a spectrum**, not vague adjectives. "Friendly" is useless; instead: "Formal ←—●———→ Casual," "Serious ←———●—→ Playful," "Reserved ←●————→ Enthusiastic," "Matter-of-fact ←——●——→ Emotive." Place the brand on each, justified by the audience and positioning. Then translate each dimension into concrete rules and, critically, **examples** — the same message written in-voice and off-voice, side by side. Examples teach voice far better than descriptors.

## Make it usable — do/don't and word lists

A voice guide only works if it's operational:

- **We say / we don't say**: a vocabulary list. Preferred terms and banned ones (e.g. say "people," not "users"; say "help," not "leverage"; never "revolutionary," "seamless," "cutting-edge"). This single tool prevents most voice drift.
- **Grammar & mechanics conventions**: contractions (usually yes, for warmth), Oxford comma, sentence-case vs title-case headings, emoji policy, exclamation-point tolerance, how to handle numbers, how formal the punctuation.
- **Do/don't pairs** for common situations: error messages, CTAs, headlines, social posts.

## Tone: flexing voice by context

Same voice, different tone by situation and reader emotion. Give explicit guidance: celebratory on a success screen, calm and plain in an error, confident on a sales page, patient in help docs, empathetic in a churn/cancellation flow. The rule: match the reader's emotional state — never chirpy in an error, never stiff in a welcome. Provide a small matrix of contexts × tone adjustments.

## Distinctiveness — avoid the beige middle

Most brand voices are indistinguishable because they optimize for inoffensiveness. Push for a real point of view: a brand can be dry and technical, or warm and irreverent, but it must be *something*. Test distinctiveness with the "cover test": cover the logo — could this copy belong to any competitor? If yes, it's too generic. A specific voice repels some people; that's the price of being memorable to the right ones.

## Application and stress-test

Prove the framework by writing sample copy across formats — a homepage headline, a product-update announcement, an error message, a social post, a sales email — all unmistakably one voice. This validates the guide is buildable and gives future writers templates. Include these in the deliverable.

## Quality bar

Acceptable: a list of adjectives and a value prop sentence. **Great**: a positioning statement with a real defensible differentiator, message pillars each backed by proof, voice defined on justified spectrums with in-voice/off-voice examples, an operational say/don't-say word list and mechanics conventions, tone guidance mapped to reader emotion, and sample copy across formats proving one coherent voice that passes the cover test. Failure modes to reject: voice defined only by vague adjectives, positioning that any competitor could claim, a value prop full of buzzwords, no examples (the part writers actually learn from), a voice so inoffensive it's invisible, and guidelines with no banned-word list to enforce them.
