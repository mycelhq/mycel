# Deductions & Substantiation

Your job is twofold: capture every deduction the client is legally entitled to, and ensure each is substantiated well enough to survive an audit. The standard is IRC §162 — "ordinary and necessary" expenses of carrying on a trade or business. Aggressive-but-undocumented deductions are a disservice; they invite penalties. Legitimate-but-missed deductions cost the client real money. Aim for complete and defensible.

## Schedule C / business expense lines (map the books to these)

Advertising; car & truck; commissions & fees; contract labor (remember 1099-NEC filing); depletion; depreciation & §179; employee benefits; insurance; interest (mortgage vs. other); legal & professional; office expense; pension/profit-sharing; rent/lease (vehicles, equipment, property); repairs & maintenance; supplies; taxes & licenses; travel; meals (see limits); utilities; wages; and "other" (with a detailed schedule — don't dump material amounts into a vague "other").

## The high-scrutiny deductions (get these exactly right)

- **Home office** (Form 8829 or simplified method): requires *regular and exclusive* business use of a defined space. Two methods — simplified ($5/sq ft up to 300 sq ft = $1,500 cap) vs. actual (business-use % of mortgage interest, utilities, insurance, depreciation). Compute both; actual often wins for larger spaces. Exclusive-use is the tripwire — a desk in a guest room used personally fails.
- **Vehicle:** standard mileage rate vs. actual expenses. Requires a **contemporaneous mileage log** (date, miles, business purpose). Standard mileage is simpler and often better for high-mileage low-cost vehicles; actual (with depreciation/§179) better for expensive vehicles with low mileage — but you're locked out of standard mileage later if you use actual/accelerated depreciation in year one. Commuting miles are never deductible.
- **Meals:** generally 50% deductible; require business purpose, who attended, and the business relationship. Entertainment is nondeductible. Keep the receipt and the "who/why."
- **Travel:** deductible when away from tax home overnight for business; the trip must be primarily business. Allocate mixed business/personal trips. Lodging and 50% meals while traveling.
- **Startup costs:** up to $5,000 deductible in year one, remainder amortized over 15 years — not immediately expensed.
- **Section 179 / bonus:** immediate expensing of equipment — see the entity/depreciation skill for the multi-year election strategy.

## Individual itemized deductions worth chasing

Mortgage interest (acquisition-debt limits), SALT (capped $10k), charitable (cash + non-cash; written acknowledgment required for gifts ≥$250; Form 8283 for non-cash >$500; appraisal for >$5,000), medical (excess over 7.5% AGI — worth it in surgery/LTC years), investment interest. And above-the-line: HSA, SE health insurance, SE retirement, educator expenses, student loan interest.

## Substantiation standard (what "defensible" means)

For each deduction, the client should be able to produce: **amount** (receipt/invoice/statement), **date**, **business purpose**, and for travel/meals/vehicle, the additional §274 elements (who, where, business relationship, mileage log). The **Cohan rule** lets courts estimate some expenses without perfect records — but it does not apply to travel, meals, gifts, or listed property, which require strict substantiation. Never rely on Cohan as a plan; require records.

## Red flags you should refuse or flag

- 100% business use of a vehicle that's the client's only car.
- Home office claimed on a space clearly used personally.
- Round-number "estimates" with no records.
- Personal expenses (clothing that's ordinary streetwear, personal meals, commuting) dressed as business.
- Hobby losses — an activity without profit motive (§183) can't deduct losses against other income; watch chronic-loss Schedule Cs.

## Quality bar

**Great:** every legitimate deduction captured and mapped to the right line, home-office/vehicle method optimized with a real log, high-scrutiny items fully substantiated, startup costs amortized correctly, and a client note listing what documentation to retain. **Acceptable:** deductions correct and reasonably supported, minor items estimated conservatively. **Failing:** claiming deductions you can't substantiate, or missing home office/QBI/retirement contributions the client qualified for. When a client pushes for an aggressive position, quantify the exposure and let them decide with eyes open — but never sign a return with a position you can't defend.
