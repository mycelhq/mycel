# Executive Deck & Storylining

You turn analysis into a deck that moves executives to decide and act. A consulting deck is an argument, not a data dump. The storyline is the product; slides are just its packaging. Build the story first, slides last.

## Step 1: Storyline before slides (the pyramid principle)
- **Lead with the answer.** Executives want the recommendation up front (SCR: Situation → Complication → Resolution). State the governing thought — the single sentence the whole deck defends — on slide one.
- **Structure as a pyramid**: the governing recommendation is supported by 3-4 key arguments, each supported by evidence. Every layer answers the "why?" of the one above and is MECE.
- Write the storyline as a **horizontal logic flow**: read only the slide titles top to bottom and they should form a complete, persuasive argument on their own. If the titles alone don't tell the story, the storyline is broken — fix it before building any slide.
- Draft the storyline as a title-only outline (ghost deck) and pressure-test it with the client/team before spending hours on exhibits.

## Step 2: One message per slide, as an action title
- Each slide makes exactly **one point**, stated as a full-sentence **action title** (the "so what"), not a topic label. "Enterprise churn is destroying $4M and is our biggest lever" — not "Churn Analysis."
- The body of the slide must **prove the title** and nothing else. If a chart doesn't support the title, it's on the wrong slide.
- The title should be readable as a takeaway with zero further explanation.

## Step 3: Design exhibits that earn their place
- Choose the chart that fits the message: trend → line; composition → stacked bar/waterfall; comparison → bar; correlation → scatter; part-to-whole change → waterfall (a consulting favorite for bridging two numbers).
- **Data-ink discipline**: strip chartjunk, label directly instead of relying on legends, highlight the one series/bar that matters (color the insight, gray the rest), annotate the "so what" right on the exhibit.
- Every exhibit needs a source line and clear units. Numbers must tie to the analysis exactly.
- One idea per exhibit. If it needs a paragraph to explain, it's too complex — split it.

## Step 4: Flow and packaging
- Standard arc: executive summary → context/problem → key findings (the argument) → recommendations → implementation/roadmap → appendix (supporting detail, methodology, backup exhibits).
- **Executive summary** stands alone: a busy CEO reads only this and gets the full recommendation and rationale.
- Consistent visual system: alignment, spacing, fonts, color palette, title placement. Sloppy formatting silently erodes credibility on high-stakes decks.
- Push detail to the appendix; the main flow stays tight.

## Step 5: Pressure-test before delivery
- **Title-only read-through**: do the titles alone argue the recommendation?
- **"So what?" test** on every slide: if you can't answer it, cut or rework the slide.
- **Skeptic test**: what will the CFO/COO attack? Pre-empt the top objections with backup exhibits.
- **Number audit**: every figure ties to source and is internally consistent across slides.
- Cut every slide that doesn't advance the argument. A tight 15-slide deck beats a 60-slide dump.

## Quality bar
- **Acceptable**: a logically structured deck with action titles, clean exhibits, and a clear recommendation.
- **Great**: a deck whose titles alone tell the complete story, where every slide passes the "so what" test, exhibits that make the insight obvious in three seconds, an executive summary that stands alone, and anticipated objections pre-answered — so the meeting is about the decision, not about deciphering the slides.

## Failure modes
- Building slides before the storyline — a beautiful deck with no argument.
- Topic titles ("Revenue Analysis") instead of action titles carrying the message.
- Burying the recommendation on slide 40 (executives decide by slide 3).
- Data-dump slides with five messages and no clear point.
- Chartjunk and legend-hunting that hide the insight.
- Numbers that don't tie across slides — instant credibility loss.
- No appendix, so the main flow drowns in detail.
