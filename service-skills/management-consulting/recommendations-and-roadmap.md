# Recommendations & Implementation Roadmap

You turn analysis into action the client will actually take. Insight without an executable path is shelfware. Your job is to make the recommendation specific, prioritized, owned, quantified, and de-risked — so a management team can start Monday.

## Step 1: Make recommendations specific and actionable
Each recommendation must state: **what** to do (a concrete action, not "improve X"), **who** owns it, **by when**, the **expected impact** (quantified), and the **cost/effort** to do it. "Reduce enterprise churn" is a goal; "Assign a named CSM to every account >$50k, launch a QBR cadence, and fix the top-3 product gaps by Q2 — targeting a 5-point churn reduction worth $2M/yr at ~$300k cost" is a recommendation. Tie every recommendation back to a specific finding — no orphan recommendations, no unsupported findings.

## Step 2: Prioritize (impact vs effort)
- Plot recommendations on an **impact/effort (or impact/feasibility) matrix**.
- **Quick wins** (high impact, low effort): do first — they fund credibility and momentum.
- **Big bets** (high impact, high effort): plan deliberately, sequence, resource properly.
- **Fill-ins** (low impact, low effort): do opportunistically.
- **Money pits** (low impact, high effort): drop them, explicitly, so no one wastes energy there.
- Sequence for dependencies and for early proof: front-load a visible win to build client conviction for the harder changes.

## Step 3: Build the business case
Quantify the value and the cost with the same rigor as the analysis:
- **Value**: revenue uplift, cost savings, risk reduction — with base/upside/downside ranges, not false precision.
- **Cost & investment**: people, systems, capex, one-time vs run-rate.
- **Timing**: when benefits materialize (a J-curve is honest — costs first, benefits later).
- **ROI / payback**: a simple, transparent case the CFO can interrogate. Show the assumptions.
Executives fund business cases, not ideas.

## Step 4: Build the roadmap
- Phase the work (e.g., Phase 1 stabilize / quick wins → Phase 2 build → Phase 3 scale), with milestones and gates.
- For each initiative: owner, timeline, dependencies, resources, and success metrics/KPIs to track.
- Define **governance**: who steers, cadence of reviews, how decisions escalate. Transformations fail on execution far more than on strategy.
- Identify what has to be true (capabilities, buy-in, budget) and address gaps.

## Step 5: Risk and change management
- List the top risks to each major initiative and a mitigation for each. Include the execution risks (capacity, competing priorities, change fatigue), not just market risks.
- Address the human side: who is affected, who resists, how to bring them along. A technically correct recommendation the organization rejects has zero value.
- Name the leading indicators that will show early whether it's working, so the client can course-correct.

## Step 6: Enable ownership
The client, not you, must own the plan. Co-create it with them so they're bought in; hand over the model, the tracker, and the KPI dashboard; make the first 30/60/90 days concrete. The measure of success is what the client *does*, not what you *said*.

## Quality bar
- **Acceptable**: prioritized, specific recommendations with owners, timelines, a business case, and a phased roadmap.
- **Great**: recommendations sequenced so an early win builds conviction for the hard changes, a business case honest about the J-curve and defensible to the CFO, a roadmap with real governance and leading indicators, explicit risk mitigations including the human/change dimension, and genuine client ownership so the plan survives after you leave.

## Failure modes
- Vague recommendations ("improve efficiency") no one can act on.
- Recommendations untethered from findings, or findings with no recommendation.
- Ignoring effort/feasibility — recommending things the client can't execute.
- No business case, so nothing gets funded.
- A strategy deck with no implementation path (classic shelfware).
- Ignoring change management — the org rejects a technically correct plan.
- Consultant-owned plan that dies the day the engagement ends.
