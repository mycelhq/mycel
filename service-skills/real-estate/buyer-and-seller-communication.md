# Buyer and Seller Communication

A real-estate transaction is the largest financial and most emotional decision most clients ever make. They are anxious, they will fixate on small things, and they judge you as much on responsiveness and clarity as on outcome. Your communication is the product. Deals fall apart from silence, surprise, and mismanaged emotion far more than from price.

## Set expectations up front

At the start of the relationship, walk the client through the whole roadmap: the phases (search/prep → offer → under contract → inspection → appraisal → financing → closing), typical timelines, who does what, and where the friction points usually are. A client who knows an inspection will surface *some* issues won't panic when it does. Agree on communication norms: how you'll reach them, how often you'll update (even "no news" updates), and your response-time commitment. Meeting a stated cadence beats sporadic brilliance.

## Responsiveness is non-negotiable

Reply fast — same day, ideally within a couple of hours during active phases. Even "I got this, I'll have an answer by 4" stops the anxiety spiral. Unreturned messages are the #1 client complaint about agents and a common reason they walk. When you're presenting an offer, coordinating a counter, or chasing a lender, minutes matter.

## Communicate for the audience

- **Sellers** want to know their home is being marketed and to feel progress. Give consistent showing reports, feedback themes, and market context. When you must deliver bad news (overpriced, low traffic, a needed repair), lead with data, not opinion, and pair the problem with a recommended action. Never let a seller discover a problem from someone else.
- **Buyers** want reassurance they're making a smart choice and not overpaying or missing out. Educate them on the market so their expectations are realistic, be honest when a home is overpriced or a fixer, and never pressure them into a house — but do create honest urgency in a competitive situation. Manage the emotional arc: excitement at the start, fear at the offer, dread at the inspection.

## Deliver hard news well

Bad news travels best when it's early, direct, framed with data, and paired with a path forward. "The appraisal came in 15k low. Here are our three options and what I recommend and why." Never sugarcoat to the point of surprise later, and never dump a problem without a plan. Frame setbacks as normal parts of the process (they usually are) so the client stays regulated. A calm, competent agent in a crisis is worth the whole commission.

## Manage emotions and negotiations

- Keep the client's emotions from sabotaging the deal. A seller insulted by a low offer wants to reject it outright; your job is to counter strategically and keep the deal alive. A buyer in a bidding war wants to overpay from fear; your job is to keep them at a number they won't regret.
- **Present all offers and material facts promptly and honestly** — this is a fiduciary and legal duty, not a judgment call. Don't filter what the client is entitled to know.
- In negotiation communication, be firm but never hostile with the other side; you may transact with the same agent for years, and a scorched relationship costs future clients. Separate the people from the problem.

## Document and confirm

Confirm important conversations and decisions in writing — verbal agreements evaporate and memories differ. Recap after key calls: "Confirming we'll counter at X and ask them to cover the repair — reply if that's not right." This protects the client and you, and prevents the "I never agreed to that" dispute. Never give legal or tax advice beyond your license — refer to the attorney/CPA and say so plainly.

## Quality bar and failure modes

Great: the client always knows what's happening and what's next, hears from you before they have to ask, gets bad news early with a plan, stays calm because you're calm, and trusts you enough to take your advice at the crux moments. They refer you afterward. Acceptable: responsive, honest, decisions documented, expectations set. Failing: going silent during a tense phase, letting a client be blindsided by a foreseeable problem, delivering bad news with no path forward, letting emotion torch a viable deal, failing to promptly present an offer or material fact, over-promising outcomes, or straying into legal/tax advice you're not licensed to give.
