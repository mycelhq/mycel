# What you may decide, and what you must ask

Bookkeeping is the one job in this system where **being confidently wrong is worse than being slow**.
A mis-sent email is embarrassing and recoverable in an hour. A misclassified transaction is
discovered by an accountant in March, has consequences with a tax authority, and costs the client
more to unpick than the whole month's bookkeeping was worth.

So the governing rule: **decide what is unambiguous, ask about everything else, and never split the
difference by guessing quietly.**

## The table

Find the row before you reason. The prose below explains why each row is where it is; the table is
what you act on, and it is the same answer every month.

| Transaction | Decide alone? | If unsure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Same merchant, same shape, already categorised correctly before | **Yes** | Match the earlier one exactly |
| Known recurring operating cost (hosting, domain, a subscription paid for months) | **Yes** | — |
| Bank, card or processor fee | **Yes** | — |
| Payment matching an invoice on amount, reference and date | **Yes** | If any of the three disagree, **ask** |
| Payroll run matching previous runs | **Yes** | Any change in people or amounts, **ask** |
| Equipment purchase (laptop, phone, tools) | **No** | Expense or asset depends on cost and use — **ask** |
| Meals, travel, entertainment | **No** | Entertainment, subsistence or personal — only they know — **ask** |
| Payment to a person rather than a company | **No** | Contractor, employee, refund or drawings — **ask** |
| Anything involving the owner personally | **No** | Always ask. Always. |
| A round-number transfer with no reference | **No** | **Ask** |
| Anything you have not seen before on this account | **No** | **Ask** |

One rule resolves every row not in the table: **if the tax treatment depends on intent, you cannot
read intent off a bank line, so you ask.**

## The other kind of guess: scope

Everything above is about guessing what a transaction IS. The second kind is guessing what you were
not given, and it is easier to miss because the arithmetic still works.

A client asked for a **quarterly** VAT return, was handed **one month** of transactions, and got a
quarterly liability computed from that month. Every figure in it added up. It was still wrong, and
the client's words were exact: *"there is no visible June or July work, so the quarterly VAT
liability is unsupported."*

So, before you compute anything that spans a period:

| You were asked for | You were given | Do this |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A return for a period | Transactions for that whole period | Compute it |
| A return for a period | Part of the period | **Do not compute it.** Say which months you hold, say what is missing, and give the figure for what you DO hold, labelled as that month and not as the return |
| A closing balance | A statement that ends on the closing date | Reconcile it |
| A closing balance | A statement ending earlier | Reconcile to the statement's own end date and say so |

**A number that is right about the wrong period is not a smaller version of the right answer.** The
client pays from it, and they cannot tell by looking that it covers a third of what it claims. This
is the same rule as never plugging a difference, applied to time instead of to money.

## Decide alone

- **A repeat of something already categorised the same way.** Same merchant, same shape, same
  account, already correct at least once. This is the majority of every month.
- **Obvious operating costs with a known vendor.** The hosting bill, the domain renewal, the
  software subscription they have paid for eleven months.
- **Bank fees, card fees, payment processor deductions.** Mechanical and unambiguous.
- **A payment matching an invoice exactly** — amount, reference, and date all consistent.
- **Payroll runs that match the previous ones.**

## Always ask

- **Anything where the tax treatment depends on INTENT.** A laptop is an expense or an asset
  depending on how they use it and how much it cost. A meal is entertainment, subsistence, or
  personal, and only the client knows which. You cannot infer intent from a bank line and you must
  not try.
- **A payment to a person rather than a company.** Contractor, employee, refund, or the owner
  drawing money — four different treatments, and getting it wrong is the error that draws attention.
- **Anything involving the owner personally.** Drawings, director's loan, expense reimbursement,
  personal spend on the business card. Always ask. Always.
- **A round number to an unfamiliar recipient.** $2,000 to a name that has never appeared before is
  either a genuine supplier or something that needs explaining, and no pattern in the data tells you
  which.
- **Anything crossing a period boundary.** An annual subscription paid in one month covers twelve,
  and whether that matters depends on how they account. Ask once; it is a decision that then holds.
- **Refunds, chargebacks and reversals.** The direction of the correction depends on what the
  original was, and a mirrored guess doubles the original error.
- **Anything you would have to caveat in the summary.** If the sentence needs "probably", it is a
  question, not a categorisation.

## Where the unresolved ones SIT while you wait

Asking is half of it. The other half is where the money goes in the meantime, and getting that wrong
undoes the asking.

**An item you would recommend excluding does not sit in operating costs.** A close flagged a $340
card payment as "owner or personal spend, held outside expenses until confirmed" — and then counted
it inside the expense total anyway. The client read both sentences and said the obvious thing:

> The headline net figure is not decision-useful because it includes $340.00 explicitly labelled
> "owner or personal spend" as an expense. If it is personal, it should be shown as drawings or owner
> account activity, not operating cost.

Take your own recommendation. If your advice is "probably personal", put it in the owner account and
say the profit figure excludes it pending confirmation — and **name that account correctly**, because
which one it is depends on the entity and the client will know if you get it wrong. A sole trader
takes **drawings**. A limited company director takes a **director's loan**, which is a debt back to
the company and carries a s455 charge if it is still outstanding nine months after the year end.
A close that wrote "drawings" while also mentioning corporation tax was told so: *"if this is a
limited company, a potentially personal payment should normally be considered for the director's loan
account, not casually called drawings."* If you do not know the entity type, that is the question —
ask it once and it holds for every future month. If your advice is "probably a
business cost", put it in the category and say so. What you must never do is recommend one treatment
and book the other — that is a document arguing with itself, and the client is the one who has to
resolve it.

**Suspense is a real place, not a hedge.** Anything genuinely unclassifiable — the unidentified card
payment, the round number to an unknown name — goes to a named suspense line with its amount, so the
client can see exactly what is unplaced and what it is worth. A suspense balance of $340 with one line
in it is honest bookkeeping. $340 buried in "software" is not.

## Entertaining: the VAT answer is not the tax answer

Client entertaining is the case where getting half of it right reads as getting it wrong. Two separate
rules apply and they are not the same rule:

- **VAT** — input VAT on client entertaining is not recoverable. Staff entertaining generally is,
  within limits.
- **Corporation tax** — client entertaining is **not deductible** against profits at all, even when it
  is a genuine and necessary business cost. Staff entertaining generally is.

A close that told a client "this lunch affects your recoverable input VAT" and stopped there was
answering the smaller half. They noticed: *"the treatment note addresses input VAT but does not tell
me the direct-tax consequence."* When you flag a meal, say both — that it is added back for
corporation tax and that the VAT is not reclaimable — because the client is deciding whether the lunch
was worth it and one of those numbers is much larger than the other.

## Ask ONCE, and ask well

The failure that makes clients hate bookkeeping software is being asked the same question every
month. So:

- **Ask about the RULE, not the transaction.** Not "how should I categorise this $48 to Acme
  Supplies?" but "Acme Supplies appears monthly — is that always office supplies?" One answer
  resolves this month and every future one.
- **Batch the questions.** One message with six questions is a ten-minute job. Six messages across a
  week is a client who stops replying — and see the touch budget: it is also six touches against a
  daily ceiling of two.
- **Give your best guess with each one.** "I have put this under software — correct?" is answerable
  in a word. "How should I treat this?" makes them do the thinking, and they hired someone so they
  would not have to.
- **Record the answer as a rule.** An answer that is not written down as a rule is a question you
  will ask again, which is the thing they will cancel over.

## The anomalies actually worth flagging

An anomaly report that lists everything unusual is noise. Flag these, and be quiet about the rest:

- **A cost that has changed materially.** Not "up 3%" — a subscription that doubled, a supplier
  whose invoice is half what it was. Something changed and they may not know.
- **A duplicate payment.** The same amount to the same recipient twice in a period is usually a real
  error and it is real money back.
- **A subscription with no corresponding use.** Software they are paying for that nobody mentions.
- **Revenue concentration.** When one client becomes most of the month's income, say so once. It is
  the single most useful thing a bookkeeper notices about a small agency, and almost none of them
  do.
- **A gap in the pattern.** A retainer that did not arrive. That is the one worth an email today
  rather than a line in a report at month end.

**Do not flag:** normal variance, anything already explained last month, or anything you have not
checked against the prior period. An anomaly the owner has to dismiss is worse than silence, because
the third one teaches them to skim.

## What the owner actually wants from the close

They are not reading a P&L for pleasure. Three things, in this order:

1. **Did I make money this month, and is that normal?** One number with one comparison.
2. **Is anything wrong?** The flags above, or explicitly nothing.
3. **What do I need to do?** Questions to answer, a bill to look at, a tax date coming.

Everything else is the file, available if they want it. A close that opens with a table of accounts
has buried the answer under the working.

## When the books do not reconcile

Say so plainly and stop. **A close that balances because something was forced is worse than an open
one**, and it is the specific failure that ends the relationship — not because the month was late,
but because they stop trusting the ones that came before it.

Name the difference, name where you looked, and ask. "Reconciled except for $340 between the bank
and the ledger, which I think is the two Stripe payouts on the 29th — can you confirm?" is a good
month's work. A silent plug is not.
