# A website is not one deliverable

The instinct is to build the site and show it. It is wrong, and it fails in a specific, expensive
way: **the client's real opinions arrive after the thing is built.**

They cannot tell you the homepage headline is wrong until they see a homepage. So they see it, they
say it, and now the change is not a sentence — it is a rebuild, because the design was made to fit
the old words and the layout was made to fit the old design.

Every experienced studio solves this the same way. They get agreement on the cheap thing before
building the expensive thing.

## Four handovers, in this order

### 1 — The copy deck and sitemap

**Before anything is designed or built.** Every page that will exist, and the actual words on each
one. Not lorem, not placeholder, not "we'll fill this in" — the real headline, the real
proposition, the real calls to action.

This is the deliverable that saves the project, and it is the one clients resist because it does not
look like progress. It is worth insisting on. Changing a headline in a document is free; changing it
after launch touches design, layout, and often the whole page.

**Done when:** they have read every page's words and said yes, or edited them. Not "no objections" —
an actual yes.

### 2 — The staging link, with a named deadline

One URL, working, on a real device. Sent with a specific date by which feedback is needed, and what
happens if it does not arrive.

**A review with no deadline is not a review.** It is an open invitation that a busy owner will
answer in three weeks, and the project sits still the whole time. "Have a look when you get a
chance" is how a two-week build becomes two months.

Say it plainly: *"Could you go through this by Thursday? Anything after that lands in the next
round rather than this one."*

### 3 — The change list, one per round

Their feedback, written back to them as a numbered list of what will change, before you change
anything.

Two reasons, and the second is the one people miss. It catches misunderstandings while they are
cheap — "make the header cleaner" means four different things and you will pick the wrong one. And
it **bounds the round**, which is what stops scope creep without a single awkward conversation. Item
eleven arriving after the list was agreed is visibly a new round, and nobody has to argue about it.

**Two rounds is the norm.** Say so at the start. A third round is a conversation about scope, not a
surprise.

### 4 — Launch and handover

The part everybody forgets, and the part that decides whether they come back.

- the live site, on their domain
- DNS and hosting explained in one paragraph, in their name, not ours
- analytics installed and actually reporting
- **a short recording of how to edit the thing they will want to edit** — usually the opening hours,
  the phone number, one page of text

That recording is the highest-value five minutes in the whole engagement. Without it every trivial
edit comes back to you forever, which feels like retention and is actually an unpaid support
contract.

## What never to hand over

- **A site with placeholder content.** They will react to the placeholder. You will then have a
  conversation about lorem ipsum instead of about their business.
- **A staging link with no deadline.** See above.
- **A design with no words.** Design without copy is decoration, and it gets approved on how it
  looks and then breaks the moment real text arrives.
- **A launch with no handover.** The site is not the deliverable; a business that can run its own
  site is.

## The general rule

**Get agreement on the cheap artifact before building the expensive one.** Copy before design.
Design before build. Staging before launch. Every stage exists so that a change costs a sentence
instead of a week.

If you are ever unsure whether something is a deliverable, ask: *can they say yes or no to this in
under ten minutes, and does it name what happens next?* If not, it is not ready to send.
