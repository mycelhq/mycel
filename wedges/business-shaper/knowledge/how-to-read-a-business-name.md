# How to read a business name

The founder's own description of what they sell is always the stronger signal — read that first and
weight it above everything here. But descriptions are often one terse line ("bookkeeping for small
shops"), and the business name is sitting right next to it carrying real information for free.

This is what a name reliably tells you, and — more importantly — what it does not.

## Signals that are usually real

- **A trade word.** "Bookkeeping", "Lettings", "Driving School", "Dental", "Recruitment". When it
  is there it is the strongest signal on the page, because people name businesses after what they
  sell. Take it at face value.
- **A legal suffix.** "Ltd", "LLC", "GmbH", "Pty" fixes the country, which fixes the tax regime,
  the currency and often the working week. Worth more than it looks.
- **"& Co", "& Partners", "Associates".** A professional-services practice: a handful of people
  billing time or retainers, not a product company.
- **A personal name alone** ("Hartley", "Okonkwo & Sons") — an owner-operated business, usually
  small, usually with the founder personally in every customer conversation. The first job should
  take work off *their* desk specifically.
- **A place name.** Local service business, geographically bounded demand, and its customers are
  almost always businesses or households in that place rather than anywhere on the internet.

## Signals that are noise

- **Abstract or invented words** — "Lumina", "Verdant", "Kestrel". These tell you the founder cared
  about branding. They tell you nothing about the business. Do not pattern-match a Latin root into a
  sector; that is how you confidently describe a plumbing firm as a wellness brand.
- **"Solutions", "Group", "Global", "Digital", "Partners" on their own.** Filler. Strip them before
  you read the rest.
- **Anything you can only reach through two inferences.** If deducing the sector took a chain of
  reasoning, the chain is the guess, not the evidence. Drop `confidence` to `low` and say what you
  assumed.

## When the name says nothing

Say so, in the shape rather than around it: `confidence: "low"`, and `assumptions` listing the one
or two things you had to decide in order to produce anything at all.

Then still produce a shape. An empty answer is worse than a wrong one here, because the founder can
correct a wrong one in a single click and cannot correct a blank page. The product shows your
assumptions next to your answer precisely so this stays honest.

## What service businesses have in common

Whatever the sector, the shape rhymes:

- Money arrives late, and someone has to ask for it.
- Customers ask the same twenty questions, and someone has to answer them.
- Something has to be reconciled, filed or closed on a fixed date.
- New customers come from a list that has to be worked, not from a website that fills itself.
- The founder is the bottleneck in every one of the above.

The first job you propose is nearly always one of those five, expressed in the concrete terms of
this particular business.

## Reading capacity, because that is what they are buying

Almost everyone signing up is a one-to-five person agency or a solo operator with more demand than
hours. They have been turning work away and avoiding hiring. That is the frame to read their
description in.

- **"We do X for Y clients" with a small number of clients** — the constraint is the founder's own
  week, not lead flow. The first job should take a recurring chore off their desk, not find them
  more customers.
- **"We're at capacity" / "we can't take on more"** — said outright surprisingly often. Take it
  literally; it names the job for you.
- **A list of services** — the one they mention first is usually the one that pays; the one they
  mention with a complaint attached is the one eating their week. Propose work on the second.

Do not write to them about "leverage", "workflows", "scaling" or "automation". Write about the
invoice that has not been paid, the client who is waiting, and the Thursday evening they get back.
