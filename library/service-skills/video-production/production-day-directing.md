# Production Day Directing

The shoot day converts the plan into footage you can actually cut. The unforgivable sins are: unusable audio, missed coverage you discover in the edit, and continuity breaks. Everything below exists to prevent those three.

## Arrive and set up

- Crew arrives ahead of call time to set up before talent. Talent standing around watching you rig lights burns their energy and your rapport.
- Run the schedule from the production book. Assign one person to own the shot list and physically check off shots as they're captured — "we got it" from memory is how coverage gets missed.
- **Sound check first, every time.** Bad audio is the #1 reason footage is unusable and the hardest to fix in post. Monitor with closed-back headphones on the recordist, not laptop speakers. Set levels peaking around -12 dBFS with headroom; never let dialogue clip. Use a lav (hidden, on the sternum) plus a boom/shotgun where possible for redundancy. Record 30 seconds of **room tone** at every location before you leave — the editor needs it to patch gaps.

## Lighting and image

- Expose for the face. Set exposure using false color or a waveform, not the LCD's brightness. Protect highlights; you can lift shadows but blown highlights are gone.
- Shoot in a flat/log profile only if you (and the colorist) will actually grade it; otherwise a good baked-in look beats a flat file nobody grades.
- Set and lock white balance per lighting setup; don't leave it on auto — it drifts shot to shot and wrecks color continuity.
- Three-point lighting as the baseline for interviews (key, fill, back/hair). Motivate the key from a plausible source. Kill mixed color temperatures (window daylight + tungsten) unless intentional.
- Frame with headroom and lead room; put eyes on the upper third. Interview subject looks just off-lens, framed slightly off-center.
- Shoot at a shutter around double the frame rate (e.g. 1/50 at 25fps) for natural motion blur.

## Coverage discipline

- For every key moment get **at least three sizes**: wide (establish), medium (context), close (emotion). This gives the editor cutting options and covers over jump cuts.
- Hold every shot for a beat before "action" and after "cut" — clean handles make editing painless.
- Get the cutaways and inserts on the list before you strike a setup. Hands, product detail, environment, reactions. The editor will thank you or curse you based on this.
- Shoot **matched action** for anything that will cut together — same action performed across sizes so cuts land on motion.

## Continuity

- Assign continuity to one person on multi-setup shoots. Photograph each setup (wardrobe, props, positions) before wrapping it. Watch eyelines and screen direction — someone looking left in the medium must look left in the close.
- Slate or verbally ID takes; keep brief circle-take notes on the good ones so the editor isn't hunting.

## Directing performance

- Talent needs direction, not just "action." Give an adjustment before each take (a verb: "reassure me," "you're impatient"). Never give line readings for testimonials.
- For real-people interviews: build rapport first, ask them to answer in full sentences that restate the question ("Our biggest challenge was…"), and stay silent after they finish — the best soundbite often comes in the pause. Ask the same question multiple ways.
- Play back the first setup on a monitor to confirm focus, exposure, and audio are actually clean before you build the day on top of it.

## Before you leave the location

Run the wrap checklist — this is non-negotiable:
- Every shot on the list checked off, or a documented decision to drop it.
- Room tone captured at each location.
- Audio spot-checked on headphones (not clipped, lav not rustling).
- Focus checked on hero shots at 100% on the monitor.
- All releases signed and collected.
- Media backed up before striking, ideally to two drives (see delivery skill — offload starts on set).

## Quality bar

- **Great:** clean, redundant audio; correctly exposed, in-focus, color-consistent footage; three sizes plus cutaways on every beat; continuity photographed; you leave with more than you need.
- **Failure:** discovering in the edit that the lav rustled, a hero shot is soft, you have no room tone, or you never got the wide. None of these are fixable remotely — they are shoot-day failures. Treat "we'll fix it in post" as a red flag, not a plan.
