# Engagement Design and Intake

A coaching engagement that starts vague ends vague. The difference between a transformative engagement and expensive conversation is set in the first two sessions: clear outcomes, a baseline, a cadence, and an explicit contract about roles and confidentiality. Design the container before you do the coaching.

## The intake: diagnose before you prescribe

Run a dedicated intake session (often 90 minutes) whose only job is to understand the client's reality, not solve anything.
- **Presenting issue vs. real issue.** The stated goal ("I want to be a better delegator") usually sits on a deeper one (fear of losing control, unclear priorities, a team they don't trust). Probe until the client names what's actually at stake. Don't accept the first framing.
- **Current state, concretely.** Ask for specific recent situations, not generalities. "Tell me about the last time this showed up" beats "how do you usually handle conflict."
- **What they've already tried** and why it didn't stick. This reveals patterns and prevents you re-prescribing failed fixes.
- **Stakes and motivation.** What happens if nothing changes? What does success unlock? Weak motivation predicts weak follow-through — surface it now.
- For executive engagements, understand the **system**: their role, their boss's expectations, their team, and the organizational context. Coaching an executive in isolation from their system produces insights that don't survive contact with Monday.

## Establish a baseline and define outcomes

You cannot show progress you never measured. Establish a baseline:
- **Qualitative:** self-assessment on the target dimensions (1-10), and where possible **stakeholder input** — for executive coaching, a lightweight 360 (interviews with boss, peers, reports) is the single highest-leverage intake tool. It replaces the client's self-story with reality and creates buy-in for change.
- **Quantitative:** tie to business metrics where the goal is business outcomes (team retention, pipeline, revenue, cycle time, engagement scores). Coaching a founder on leadership should ultimately move something the business can see.

Convert into 2-3 outcome goals, not ten. Each must be:
- **Outcome-framed** ("my direct reports run their own decisions without escalating to me"), not activity-framed ("delegate more").
- **Observable** — someone other than the client could tell it happened.
- **Owned by the client** — they chose it, in their words. Imposed goals don't move.

Write them down and make them the spine of every future session.

## Design the cadence and structure

- **Duration:** most meaningful behavior change needs 3-6 months minimum. Real habit and identity change rarely happens in fewer than ~6 sessions; be honest that a one-off is a conversation, not an engagement.
- **Frequency:** typically every 1-2 weeks. Weekly early (momentum), stretching to biweekly as habits form. The gap between sessions is where the work happens — don't schedule so tight there's no room to practice, or so loose that accountability evaporates.
- **Session length:** 45-60 minutes for standing sessions, longer for intake and quarterly reviews.
- **Between-session contact:** define it. Some engagements include async check-ins (a weekly written reflection, a short message when stuck); others are session-only. Set the boundary explicitly so it's not renegotiated every week.
- **Review points:** schedule a mid-engagement and end-of-engagement review to re-measure against baseline.

## Contract explicitly

Coaching fails on unspoken assumptions. Make a clear agreement covering:
- **Roles:** you are a thinking partner and accountability structure, not a consultant who does the work or a therapist. Coaching is forward-looking and action-oriented; if the client presents clinical mental-health needs, refer out — know that boundary.
- **Confidentiality:** what's said stays between you. For sponsored executive coaching (the company pays), define upfront exactly what, if anything, is reported to the sponsor — usually only theme-level progress and attendance, never content. Get this in writing; it protects the trust the whole engagement runs on.
- **Client responsibilities:** show up prepared, do the between-session commitments, be honest. Coaching only works on a willing, active client — name this.
- **Logistics:** cancellation policy, rescheduling, fees, and what ends the engagement.

## Quality bar

- **Great:** you finish intake with a concrete baseline (ideally stakeholder-informed), 2-3 client-owned, observable outcome goals, a right-sized cadence, defined between-session work and boundaries, and a written contract with airtight confidentiality. The engagement has a spine and a way to prove it worked.
- **Failure:** starting real coaching with no baseline, fuzzy activity-goals, no measurement, an undefined sponsor-reporting boundary, or a client who never explicitly agreed to do the work. Undesigned engagements drift into pleasant, unaccountable chat — the most common way coaching wastes a client's money.
