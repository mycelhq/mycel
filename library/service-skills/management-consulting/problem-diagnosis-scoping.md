# Problem Diagnosis & Scoping

You convert a client's vague ask ("sales are down," "we need to grow," "costs are too high") into a precise problem statement and a workplan. Most consulting failures are scoping failures — solving the wrong problem well. Spend disproportionate effort here; it determines everything downstream.

## Step 1: Sharpen the question
The presented problem is rarely the real one. Pin down:
- **The exact question to answer**: not "improve profitability" but "which two levers will restore EBITDA margin to 15% within 12 months without new capital?" A good problem statement is specific, measurable, bounded in scope and time, and tied to a decision the client will actually make.
- **The decision-maker and the decision**: who acts on this, and what will they do differently depending on the answer? If no decision hinges on it, don't analyze it.
- **Constraints and success criteria**: budget, timeline, no-go zones, what "solved" looks like.
- **Why now**: the trigger reveals the real stakes.
Use the SCQ frame (Situation → Complication → Question) to write a one-paragraph problem statement and get the client to confirm it. Getting this wrong wastes the whole engagement.

## Step 2: Structure the problem (issue tree)
Decompose the question into a MECE (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive) issue tree — sub-questions that don't overlap and together cover the whole problem. Two tree types:
- **Diagnostic tree**: breaks down *why* (e.g., profit = revenue − cost; revenue = volume × price; drill each branch). Good when finding a root cause.
- **Solution/lever tree**: breaks down *what could we do* (the options). Good when the cause is known and you're choosing action.
Use known frameworks as scaffolding, not answers: profit tree, value chain, customer decision journey, 3Cs, Porter's Five Forces, 7S — but always tailor to the client's actual business. A generic framework dumped on a specific problem is a hallmark of weak work.

## Step 3: Hypothesize
Be hypothesis-driven, not boil-the-ocean. For each branch, state a testable hypothesis about the answer ("we believe the margin loss is driven ~70% by discounting in the enterprise segment, not by COGS"). This focuses analysis on what would confirm or kill the hypothesis, saving weeks. It's fine — expected — to be wrong; the point is to test fast and update.

## Step 4: Build the workplan
For each hypothesis, define: the analysis needed to test it, the data required, the source (client data, interviews, expert calls, benchmarks), the owner, and the deadline. Sequence to test the highest-stakes / most-likely-to-be-wrong hypotheses first — kill bad branches early. Identify the "so what if true" for each: if a hypothesis holds, does it actually change the recommendation? If not, deprioritize.

## Step 5: Contract on scope
Confirm with the client: the problem statement, what's in and out of scope, the key questions, the timeline, and what the final deliverable will decide. Manage scope creep actively — new questions get logged and traded against the plan, not silently absorbed.

## Quality bar
- **Acceptable**: a specific problem statement, a MECE issue tree, hypotheses, and a workplan the client signed off on.
- **Great**: a problem statement that reframes the client's ask into the *real* question (the reframe itself is often the highest-value moment of the engagement), a genuinely MECE tree tailored to their business, prioritization that front-loads the decisive analyses, and clear "so what" logic so no effort is spent on branches that can't change the answer.

## Failure modes
- Accepting the client's framing at face value and solving the stated (wrong) problem.
- Boiling the ocean — analyzing everything because no hypothesis focuses the work.
- Non-MECE trees (overlapping or gap-ridden branches) that miss the real driver.
- Framework worship — forcing a business into Porter/7S without tailoring.
- Vague success criteria, so "done" is never definable and scope creeps endlessly.
- Analysis that, even if completed, wouldn't change any decision.
