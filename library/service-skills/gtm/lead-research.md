# Researching a lead before you reach out

The difference between a message that gets a reply and one that gets deleted is usually 60 seconds of research. You are looking for one true, specific thing about this person or their company that you can reference so the opener could only have been written to them. You do not need a dossier — you need one hook.

## What to look for, in priority order

1. **Their role and its real tension.** Not the title — what the title actually means day to day at a company this size and stage. A "Head of Ops" at a 20-person startup and at a 2,000-person company live different lives. Reference the life, not the label.
2. **A recent, specific signal.** A role change, a hire they're making, a post they wrote, a launch, a funding round, an opening on their team. Recent signals are gold because they imply a current priority — and a message that lands on a current priority gets answered.
3. **The company's moment.** Growth stage, an obvious initiative, a public change. This tells you what they likely care about right now.
4. **A genuine point of connection.** Shared background, a mutual, the same city, a real overlap. Use only if true.

## Where it comes from

Most of this is already in the enriched profile and the company data on file — headline, role, company, location, industry. Use what's there before spending anything. Only reach for deeper (paid enrichment, a page read) when the lead is clearly worth it: a strong ICP fit, a decision-maker, a company in the sweet spot. Cheap-and-good beats thorough-and-slow at outreach scale; the point is one hook, not a profile.

## Turn research into a hook, not a recital

The goal is ONE line the opener can lean on, not a paragraph proving you did homework. "Saw you're hiring your first RevOps person" is a hook. Listing their last three jobs is creepy. Reference the thing lightly, as a person would, and get to a real question fast.

## Judge whether they're even worth it

Part of research is qualification. If the lead is clearly outside the ICP — wrong role, wrong company size, wrong industry — the right move is to skip them, not to write a clever opener to the wrong person. A tight list that reflects real fit beats a big list every time: reply rates, sender reputation, and the founder's time all depend on it.

## Great vs acceptable vs failing

- **Great:** one specific, recent, true hook tied to a current priority of exactly this person, cheaply found, used lightly. The opener writes itself.
- **Acceptable:** grounded in role + company context that's true of this person, even if not recent or unique.
- **Failing:** no research (a blank-template opener), a creepy recital of everything you found, or reaching out to an obvious non-fit because they were on the list. Researching the wrong person well is still the wrong person.
