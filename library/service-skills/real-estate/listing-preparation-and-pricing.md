# Listing Preparation and Pricing

You are taking a home to market on behalf of the seller. The listing's first two weeks and its first weekend are decisive — the market is most attentive to a fresh listing, and a mispriced or poorly-presented home burns that attention and then stigmatizes with growing days-on-market. Get price, prep, and presentation right before it goes live, because you rarely get a second first impression.

## Price from data, not from hope

Build a Comparative Market Analysis (CMA), not a guess:

- **Pull true comparables**: recently *sold* (last 3–6 months), same submarket, similar beds/baths, square footage within ~10–15%, similar lot, age, condition, and style. Sold comps set value; active listings set your competition; pending comps show where the market is heading. Expired listings show where it's overpriced.
- **Adjust** each comp for differences: renovated kitchen, extra bath, finished basement, view, garage, updated systems. Adjust the comp toward the subject, and be honest — sellers' "upgrades" often add less than they think.
- **Read the market temperature**: months of inventory, list-to-sale ratios, and average DOM tell you if it's a seller's, balanced, or buyer's market, which sets your pricing aggressiveness.
- **Price to the search grid and to psychology.** Land just under round-number search breakpoints (e.g. list at 499,000 or 500,000 to capture the "under 500k" search bucket rather than 505,000). Pricing slightly below a hot comp to trigger competing offers often nets more than pricing at the top and chasing the market down.

Present the seller a defensible range with the recommended list price and the reasoning. Never let a seller's emotional anchor set a price the data won't support — overpricing is the number-one cause of a stale listing and a lower eventual sale.

## Prepare the property

Walk the home as a buyer would and drive prep in priority order (highest ROI first):

- **Declutter and depersonalize** — remove excess furniture, family photos, and clutter so buyers picture themselves there. This is free and hugely effective.
- **Deep clean** every surface, and address odors (pets, smoke) — smell kills deals faster than almost anything.
- **Fix the cheap, visible defects**: leaky faucets, cracked plates, burned-out bulbs, sticking doors, scuffed paint. Fresh neutral paint is the highest-ROI cosmetic upgrade.
- **Curb appeal**: the exterior is the first photo and the first in-person impression — tidy landscaping, clean entry, fresh mulch.
- **Stage** (physical or virtual) the key rooms: living, primary bedroom, kitchen. Staged homes photograph better and sell faster.
- Advise on **pre-listing inspection** for older homes so surprises don't blow up the deal later.

## Presentation and launch

- **Professional photography is mandatory** — most buyers see the home online first, and listing photos drive showing requests. Include wide, well-lit shots, twilight exterior if it flatters, floor plan, and video/3D tour for higher-value or remote-buyer homes. Never post dark, cluttered phone photos.
- **Write the listing to sell**: lead with the strongest feature and the lifestyle, name concrete upgrades with years ("roof 2021, HVAC 2022"), state what's nearby (schools, transit, amenities), and be accurate — misrepresenting square footage or features invites liability. Fill every MLS field correctly; blank fields cost you in filtered searches.
- **Time the launch**: list to be live and photogenic for the peak browsing window (typically midweek so it's fresh for the weekend), and hold showings/open house to concentrate early demand.
- **Verify the data**: correct square footage, lot size, taxes, HOA, disclosures, and school zones. An error here erodes trust and can void a contract.

## Quality bar and failure modes

Great: priced on solid comps to the search grid, prepped and staged so it shows flawlessly, professional media, launched into peak attention, and it draws strong early traffic and offers within the first days. Acceptable: fair price, clean and photographed, accurate MLS entry, reasonable DOM. Failing: pricing to the seller's ego, dark phone photos, cluttered or dirty showings, inaccurate listing data, a launch that squanders the fresh-listing window, or ignoring curb appeal and odors.

Track early signals: strong showing volume but no offers usually means the price is ~5–10% high; low showing volume means the price or the photos are wrong. Recommend a price adjustment early and decisively rather than letting DOM pile up.
