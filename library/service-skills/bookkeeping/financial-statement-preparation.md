# Financial Statement Preparation

The financial package is the product the client pays for — the close is the plumbing behind it. Your job is to turn a correct trial balance into three tied-out statements plus a narrative that a non-accountant owner can read in five minutes and make decisions from.

## The three statements and how they tie

1. **Income Statement (P&L).** Revenue → COGS → gross profit → operating expenses → operating income → other income/expense → net income. Present month-actual and YTD side by side, ideally with prior-year comparatives and % of revenue columns. Gross margin and net margin should be visible, not buried.
2. **Balance Sheet.** Assets = Liabilities + Equity, always. It must balance to the penny. Current vs. long-term classification matters (current = within 12 months). Retained earnings + current-period net income should reconcile; the net income on the P&L must equal the net income flowing into equity.
3. **Statement of Cash Flows.** Reconciles net income (accrual) to actual cash movement, in three sections: operating (indirect method — start with net income, add back non-cash items like depreciation, adjust for working-capital changes in A/R, A/P, inventory), investing (asset purchases/sales), financing (loans, owner contributions/draws). The ending cash here must equal the balance sheet cash. This statement is what reveals a "profitable but broke" client.

## Tie-out checklist before you present

- Net income on P&L = net income used in cash flow statement = change in retained earnings on balance sheet.
- Cash on balance sheet = ending cash on statement of cash flows = sum of reconciled bank balances.
- A/R on balance sheet = A/R aging total; A/P = A/P aging total.
- Accumulated depreciation and fixed assets match the depreciation schedule.
- Prior-period balances match last month's issued statements (nothing silently changed a closed period).

## Flux / variance analysis (this is the value-add)

Do not hand over numbers without explanation. For every material line, compare to prior month and same month last year, and to budget if one exists. Explain any swing beyond your threshold (e.g., >10% and >$500, scaled to client size). Examples of good narrative:
- "Revenue up 22% MoM driven by two new retainer clients starting in the period."
- "Payroll spiked $8k due to a three-payroll month (calendar timing), not a headcount change."
- "Gross margin fell from 61% to 54%; materials cost rose on the Q3 supplier price increase — recommend revisiting pricing."

## Presentation quality

- **Accrual basis for management reporting** unless the client explicitly runs cash basis; note the basis on the statements. Many SMBs file taxes cash-basis but should manage on accrual — surface both if relevant.
- Round to whole dollars for readability; keep the underlying detail exact.
- Include KPIs the owner cares about: gross margin %, net margin, current ratio, months of runway (cash ÷ monthly burn), revenue per employee, A/R days outstanding.
- Lead the package with a one-paragraph plain-English summary: how the month went, what changed, what needs a decision.

## Quality bar

- **Great:** all three statements tie perfectly, comparatives and % columns present, flux analysis with real causal explanations, KPI dashboard, and a decision-oriented narrative flagging the 1–3 things the owner should act on.
- **Acceptable:** three tied statements, MoM comparison, brief notes on big swings.
- **Failing:** a balance sheet that doesn't balance, statements that disagree on net income or cash, or a data dump with no interpretation.

## Common failure modes

- Presenting on cash basis while the client thinks it's accrual (or vice versa) — always label the basis.
- A balance sheet out of balance by a small amount, "fixed" with a rounding plug — find the real cause.
- Reporting net income as if it were cash — the cash flow statement exists precisely to prevent this misread.
- Burying a margin collapse or a runway problem inside tidy-looking totals. Your narrative exists to make the owner see it.
