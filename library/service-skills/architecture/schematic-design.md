# Schematic Design

Schematic Design (SD) is the first design phase after pre-design/programming. You turn the client's needs, the site's realities, the budget, and the code envelope into a clear spatial concept: plans, sections, massing, and the organizing idea. Decisions made here cascade through every later phase and dollar — a flaw baked into SD is expensive to fix in Construction Documents and ruinous in construction. Get the fundamentals right before the drawings get pretty.

## Start from program, site, and budget — simultaneously

- **Program**: confirm the required spaces, their sizes (net square footage), adjacencies (what must be near what), and priorities. Build or validate a program matrix and a bubble/adjacency diagram before drawing walls. Distinguish must-haves from wants so value-engineering later has a map.
- **Site**: analyze orientation (sun path for daylight and passive heating/cooling), prevailing wind, views to frame and views to screen, topography and drainage, access and parking, existing trees/features, and adjacent structures. Overlay the zoning envelope: setbacks, height limit, lot coverage / floor-area ratio (FAR), and parking requirements. The site and zoning together define the buildable envelope — design within it, or plan for a variance early.
- **Budget**: keep a rough cost target from day one. Size and massing decisions are the biggest cost levers; a design the client can't afford is a failure no matter how elegant. Sanity-check gross area × a realistic cost-per-square-foot against the budget continuously, not at the end.

## Develop the concept

- **Establish a parti** — a clear organizing idea (a spine, a courtyard, stacked public/private zones, a served-vs-servant split à la Kahn). Everything should reinforce it. A design without a governing idea reads as a pile of rooms.
- **Massing and section, not just plan.** Study the building in three dimensions early — floor-to-floor heights, roof form, how light enters, how spaces connect vertically. Many novices over-invest in plan and discover section problems too late.
- **Circulation**: resolve how people move through — entry sequence, horizontal and vertical circulation, egress. Good circulation is invisible; bad circulation (dead-end corridors, cramped stairs, crossing public and service flows) plagues the building forever.
- **Structural and systems logic**: rough in a viable structural grid and where major MEP (mechanical rooms, shafts, plumbing stacks) will live. You don't engineer it now, but the scheme must be structurally and mechanically plausible. A beautiful plan with no place for ductwork or an unbuildable span is not schematic design, it's fiction.
- **Daylight, energy, and envelope**: orient glazing and openings for daylight and thermal performance; set the basic envelope strategy. Passive moves made now cost nothing and save energy forever.

## Test against code and accessibility early

Even at SD, screen for the code decisions that shape the scheme: occupancy classification, construction type and allowable area/height, required egress (number and separation of exits, travel distance), fire separations, and accessibility (ADA / accessible route, clearances, restroom counts). These aren't CD-phase details — they can force a stair, a corridor width, or an elevator that reshapes the plan. Catching them now is cheap; discovering them in permitting is not.

## Deliverables and client sign-off

SD deliverables typically include: site plan, floor plans, key sections and elevations, a massing model or 3D views, a basic materials/character direction, and a preliminary area tabulation and outline cost estimate. Present the concept with the reasoning — why this parti, how it serves their program, how it fits site and budget. Give the client real options where meaningful, but with a recommendation. **Secure explicit written sign-off on the SD scheme before advancing** to Design Development — later-phase changes to fundamentals are the classic budget and schedule killer.

## Quality bar and failure modes

Great: a clear, defensible parti that satisfies the program, exploits the site, respects the zoning envelope, stays within budget, is structurally and mechanically plausible, screens clean against major code/egress/accessibility drivers, and the client signs off understanding it. Acceptable: a coherent scheme meeting program and code with a credible cost check. Failing: pretty renderings with no organizing idea, a plan that ignores section/MEP/structure, exceeding the zoning envelope or budget unnoticed, deferring code and accessibility to later phases, or advancing without client sign-off on fundamentals.
