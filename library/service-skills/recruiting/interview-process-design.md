# Interview Process Design

You design the interview loop for a role and orchestrate the debrief. A good loop is structured, efficient, fair, and predictive; a bad one is a series of redundant unstructured chats that measure charisma. Your job is to maximize signal per hour of everyone's time.

## Step 1: Define the scorecard first
Before designing any interview, define what you're measuring. Derive 4-6 core competencies from the role brief (mix of hard skills, role-specific judgment, and ways-of-working). For each, write what "meets the bar" looks like concretely. This scorecard is the backbone — every interview maps to specific competencies, and no competency should be measured by zero interviews or by all of them.

## Step 2: Design the loop
- **Stages**: recruiter screen → hiring manager screen → skills/work-sample → onsite loop (3-5 focused interviews) → references → offer. Cut redundant stages ruthlessly; each stage must add unique signal.
- **Assign competencies to interviews** so coverage is complete and non-redundant. Give each interviewer a clear focus ("you own system design + technical depth"), not "general fit."
- **Prefer work samples**: a realistic work sample (a scoped take-home, a live problem mirroring real work, a portfolio walk-through) is the most predictive single signal. Keep take-homes short (≤2-3 hrs) and respect candidates' time; offer to pay for longer ones.
- **Structured questions**: give each interviewer specific competency-based questions and a rubric. Behavioral ("tell me about a time…") probes past behavior; situational ("how would you approach…") probes reasoning. Insist on evidence-seeking follow-ups.
- **Logistics**: minimize elapsed time (fast loops win candidates), brief every interviewer on the role and their focus, and stagger so no two interviewers ask the same thing.

## Step 3: Run structured interviews
Coach interviewers to:
- Ask the same core questions across candidates (comparability).
- Talk ~20%, listen ~80%; dig into specifics; separate observation from judgment.
- Take contemporaneous notes tied to competencies.
- Avoid leading questions, brain-teasers, and culture-"fit" vibes (which smuggle in bias) — use **culture-add** and values-based evaluation instead.
- Sell the role in the last few minutes if the candidate is strong.

## Step 4: Independent scoring, THEN debrief
- Every interviewer submits their scorecard and hire/no-hire recommendation with evidence **before** the group discusses. This prevents anchoring on the loudest or most senior voice.
- In the debrief, go competency by competency. Surface disagreements — they're the most informative signal. "You saw strong ownership, they didn't — what specifically did each of you observe?" Reconcile with evidence, not seniority or volume.
- Watch for bias: halo effect, similar-to-me, recency, one dazzling answer overshadowing a pattern of gaps.
- Reach a clear decision: hire / no-hire / need-more-data. "Weak yes because pipeline is thin" is a trap — hire against the bar, not against desperation.

## Step 5: Close the loop
Feed structured feedback to the recruiter for the offer/decline. Give declined candidates a prompt, respectful outcome. Capture what the loop got right/wrong to improve calibration over time.

## Quality bar
- **Acceptable**: a scorecard-driven loop with assigned competencies, structured questions, independent scoring, and an evidence-based debrief.
- **Great**: additionally a loop tuned to the fewest stages that fully cover the scorecard, a realistic work sample as the centerpiece, interviewers who came in briefed and left evidence-rich notes, a debrief that mined disagreements instead of averaging them, and fast elapsed time so top candidates don't drop.

## Failure modes
- No scorecard — interviews measure charisma and overlap randomly.
- Everyone asks "so tell me about yourself" (five redundant unstructured chats).
- Group debrief before independent scores → anchoring and groupthink.
- "Culture fit" as a proxy for "like me."
- Endless loops and slow scheduling that lose the best candidates to faster competitors.
- Lowering the bar because the pipeline is thin instead of fixing sourcing.
